## 1 Framing and rationale

Sinkholes and dolines — closed depressions formed by the collapse or gradual
subsidence of ground above a void — are a well-studied geohazard across a
range of lithological and anthropogenic settings: evaporite karst, such as
the rapidly forming collapse sinkholes along the retreating Dead Sea
shoreline; classic carbonate tower karst (turmkarst) landscapes, such as
those of southern China; and anthropogenic collapse above man-made cavities
such as mines and tunnels. On the Zugspitzplatt, the high-alpine karst
plateau beneath the summit of the Zugspitze (2962 m, Germany's highest
peak) at roughly 2000 m elevation, dolines have so far mainly attracted
attention as Holocene climate archives: Grüger and Jerz (2011) excavated and
pollen/AMS-dated the sediment fill of one such feature to 7415 ± 30 BP and
used it to reconstruct regional vegetation history. What has received
comparatively little attention is the doline population itself as a
geomorphological and geohazard phenomenon in its own right — how many there
are, where they occur, how they are changing, and what is driving that
change.

The terms *doline* and *sinkhole* are used near-synonymously in the
literature — doline the term preferred in the European karst-geomorphology
tradition, sinkhole more common in North American and applied hazard
contexts — so little hinges on which word is used. What does matter is
genetic type: Gutiérrez et al. (2008) classify sinkholes by the material
collapsing (cover, bedrock, or caprock) and by mechanism (collapse,
suffosion, or sagging), a framework developed on evaporite paleokarst but
applicable regardless of lithology. Which genetic type(s) are present on
the Zugspitzplatt is one of the questions this thesis addresses.

A further, distinct mechanism relevant to this site is thermokarst:
subsidence or collapse produced by the melting of ice-rich ground,
independent of any dissolution. Regional alpine permafrost is essentially
absent from the Zugspitzplatt plateau itself (Gude and Barsch, 2005), but
Gude and Barsch document one directly relevant precedent: subsidence in the
rack-railway tunnel where it cuts through frozen debris inside a doline on
the plateau, serious enough to require pumping concrete into the resulting
void to stabilise the tunnel. This indicates that, at least locally, doline
floors can act as cold-air traps sustaining relict ground ice long after
regional permafrost has retreated — raising the central open question this
thesis addresses: are the Zugspitzplatt's dolines driven by karst
dissolution, by the thaw of such locally trapped relict ice, or by both,
potentially acting on different features, or at different stages of a
single feature's history?

Recent work also sharpens the dissolution side of this question beyond a
generic "temperature drives dissolution" model. Küfmann (2013), from
lysimeter measurements taken directly in the Zugspitzplatt's subnival zone,
found solution rates to be limited by atmospheric CO2 supply through the
snowpack rather than by meltwater temperature, peaking only once snow cover
thins late in the season. Wetzel (2004) independently confirms that the
karst system draining the plateau (the Partnach spring) is unusually fast
and active, with dolines explicitly recognised as its recharge points. A
further piece of structural evidence has only recently become available:
Ortner and Kilian (2022) mapped and named three fault sets crossing the
Zugspitzplatt directly (the Puitental, Loisach, and Ammer sets), offering a
first opportunity to test whether doline location or orientation is
structurally controlled.

several features, consistent with active mechanical weathering operating
alongside any dissolution or thermokarst process. Together, this argues
against treating the Zugspitzplatt dolines as a single, homogeneous set of
similar small closed depressions addressable by one detection method, and
for a dedicated study combining a genuine detection/mapping effort with a
driver-focused geomorphological investigation of change.

Beyond the question of origin, understanding this doline population also
matters practically: several mapped features lie in close proximity to the
marked hiking routes crossing the plateau, and repeat imagery already shows
some features changing shape between surveys — both a live geohazard
consideration and a direct motivation for quantifying change rather than
only mapping presence. The multi-epoch, multi-sensor UAV dataset available
for this site (Section 3) is also detailed enough to go beyond simple
presence/absence mapping and characterise doline shape directly — using,
for instance, the parametric cross-sectional classification of Péntek et
al. (2007), or by comparing measured diameter, depth, and wall slope
against published alpine baselines (Veress, 2017). This thesis treats that
characterisation as a secondary aim supporting both research questions
below, rather than as a standalone one.

The main research questions are therefore:

- **RQ1:** How many dolines are present on the Zugspitzplatt, where are
  they located, how are they spatially distributed, and how can they be
  identified and mapped using remote sensing?
- **RQ2:** How are these dolines changing over time, and which of the
  candidate drivers — karst dissolution timing, frost weathering, or the
  thaw of locally trapped relict ground ice — best explains that change?
