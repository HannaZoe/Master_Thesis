# Literature review: doline reactivation, snow-doline interactions, frost weathering, and repeat-survey precedents

Compiled for the Zugspitzplatt doline-dynamics thesis. Scope per brief: (1) reactivation/renewed activity of old dolines, (2) snow-doline interactions (trapping, cold-air pooling, rock-snow interface effects), (3) frost weathering specific to karst/doline walls, (4) repeat UAV/LiDAR/TLS surveys of doline change. Sources are grouped roughly by topic but several span more than one.

Each entry states how the source was accessed. Sources marked **[full text read]** were retrieved and read in essentially complete form (PDF or fetched page). Sources marked **[abstract/summary only, via indexed search snippets]** could not be opened directly (paywalled, 403-blocked) — their bibliographic details are cross-confirmed across multiple independent indexes (ADS, ScienceDirect listing, ResearchGate, Semantic Scholar), and the content summary is built from repeated, consistent search-engine-extracted abstract text, but I was not able to verify the full argument or methodology first-hand. Treat these with appropriate caution and re-verify via institutional access before citing in the thesis itself.

## 1. The dated doline itself — baseline context

**Grüger, E. & Jerz, H. (2010): Untersuchung einer Doline auf dem Zugspitzplatt – Ein palynologischer Beitrag zur holozänen Gletschergeschichte im Wettersteingebirge. *E&G Quaternary Science Journal*, 59(1–2), 66–75. DOI: 10.3285/eg.59.1-2.06.** **[full text read, open access, CC-BY]**

*Summary:* This is the source of the 7415 ± 30 BP date cited in the thesis brief (AMS date KIA 38359 on sediment from 69–72 cm depth, calibrated 6378–6229 cal BC). The paper excavated and pollen-analysed a ~1 m deep, humus-rich colluvial infill in a doline at 2290 m a.s.l. (~120 m south of the "Weißes Tal" lift station), interpreting the basal sediment as dating to the early Atlantic period (Holocene thermal optimum). Critically for the thesis, the paper's own synthesis (section 9, points 2 and 5) explicitly frames the doline's history as one of **continued, progressive processes since the early Atlantic**: "(2) Verwitterung mit intensiver Bodenbildung und Verkarstung im Präboreal ab ca. 11500 cal. BP" and "(5) Fortschreitende Verkarstung und Auffüllung der untersuchten Doline... mit humosem Kolluvium aus teils äolischem und teils eingeschwemmtem Feinmaterial seit dem frühen Atlantikum" — i.e., the authors themselves describe the doline as still actively accumulating wash-in sediment and undergoing "progressing karstification" through the entire observed record, not as a static relict form. They also note (citing Küfmann 2002/03) that aeolian dust deposition on the Zugspitzplatt is negligible in winter and only ~17 µm/year in summer, meaning most of the doline's younger fill arrived by slopewash/erosion rather than airfall — implying an active surface-process regime around the doline, consistent with a landform that keeps responding to contemporary conditions rather than being frozen in its Holocene-optimum configuration.

*Relevance:* This is not new evidence of *recent* reactivation, but it is the direct primary source for the age claim central to the thesis's framing ("old landform, recent change"), and it independently supports the idea that infilling/erosion around this doline has been continuous rather than a one-off event — useful for arguing that "old" does not mean "inactive," and for citing the age correctly (note: commonly mis-cited as "2011"; the actual publication year is 2010).

## 2. Doline reactivation and renewed growth of old dolines (Topic 1)

**Mix, C. & Küfmann, C. (2012): Genesis and controlling factors of dolines in subalpine karst, Northern Calcareous Alps (Plateau Zahmer Kaiser, Austria). *Zeitschrift für Geomorphologie*, 56(2), 141–163. DOI: 10.1127/0372-8854/2011/0065.** **[abstract and detailed findings read via publisher (Schweizerbart) abstract page]**

*Summary:* This is the single most directly relevant precedent found for the thesis's core question. The authors re-surveyed dolines on the Zahmer Kaiser plateau (1730–1750 m, Tyrol) and compared them against an original field mapping from **1911** — i.e., over a ~100-year baseline, not the thesis's 1–2 year one, but addressing exactly the same phenomenon: are old dolines static, or do some keep changing? They found **two contrasting trajectories among dolines of the same age/origin**: (1) "progressive transformation" — measurable growth in diameter and depth — occurring specifically where **long-lasting snowpatches** persist and generate sustained meltwater activity (flagged in the field by indicator vegetation, *Arabidetum caeruleae*, associated with high meltwater throughput); versus (2) "stagnation" — growth is blocked — where substratum infilling dominates (soil accumulation, sediment deposition, aeolian/loess input), which chokes off further deepening/widening regardless of meltwater supply. In other words, doline reactivation in this system is controlled by the **balance between snowmelt-driven corrosion/erosion and sediment infilling**, not by age.

*Relevance:* Directly analogous mechanism to what the thesis needs to argue: a population of old, common-origin dolines on a limestone plateau show *differential* recent morphological activity depending on local snow persistence and meltwater throughput, independent of doline age. This gives a citable precedent for "renewed doline growth is snowmelt-triggered, not thermokarst- or dissolution-clock-driven" and for treating sediment infill as the main *counteracting*, stabilising process — worth checking explicitly whether the Zugspitzplatt dolines showing change have less infill/more exposed rock than the stable ones.

**Veress, M. (2017): Solution doline development on glaciokarst in alpine and Dinaric areas. *Earth-Science Reviews*, 173, 31–48. DOI: 10.1016/j.earscirev.2017.08.008 (ScienceDirect record; DOI reconstructed from journal/volume/page, verify before citing).** **[abstract/summary only, via indexed search snippets — I could not open the full text or verify the abstract first-hand; ScienceDirect, SSRN-style mirrors and ResearchGate all 403-blocked WebFetch]**

*Summary (as far as verifiable from repeated, consistent search-engine extracts):* A synthesis of solution-doline development on glaciokarst in the Alps and Dinarides, distinguishing giant solution dolines (developed under temperate climate, typically below/near the treeline) from small-sized solution dolines and schachtdolines. For the latter, the recurring, consistently-worded finding across multiple independent search summaries is that **doline deepening is driven by meltwater from long-lasting snow patches and snowdrifts, and that this meltwater works preferentially downward (rather than laterally) because rock debris at the doline floor blocks lateral drainage** — and that deepening and enhanced snow accumulation are described as **mutually reinforcing** (a deeper doline traps more snow, which drives more meltwater-focused dissolution/erosion, which deepens it further).

*Relevance:* If accurate, this "self-reinforcing" feedback (deepening → more snow trapping → more meltwater → more deepening) is an important mechanistic hypothesis for why *some* old Zugspitzplatt dolines might show disproportionate recent change relative to neighbours of similar age — worth pursuing as a testable idea (does doline depth/morphometry predict which ones are actively changing?). However, because I could not read the actual text, do not cite this beyond the general concept without checking the original (Earth-Science Reviews, via institutional access) — the specific wording above is a paraphrase of search-engine-extracted text, not a verified quotation.

## 3. Snow-doline interactions: trapping, cold-air pooling, rock/snow-interface dissolution (Topic 2)

**Küfmann, C. (2014): Solution dynamics at the rock/snow interface during the ablation period in the subnival karst of the Wetterstein Mountains (Northern Calcareous Alps, Germany). *Zeitschrift für Geomorphologie*, 58(1), 37–57. DOI: 10.1127/0372-8854/2013/0121.** **[abstract and key quantitative findings read via publisher (Schweizerbart) abstract page]**

*Summary:* This is almost certainly the paper the thesis brief refers to as "Küfmann 2013" — the DOI itself is registered under "/2013/" (i.e., accepted/submitted in 2013, formally published in the 2014 volume), which explains the year discrepancy. It studies limestone dissolution beneath snow in the subnival zone (2350–2600 m) of the Wetterstein Mountains — the same mountain range/plateau system as the Zugspitzplatt — using lysimeters at the rock/snow interface to track meltwater CO2 content (the primary control on water acidity/dissolution capacity) through the ablation season. Key quantitative findings: interface meltwater carries only about **one quarter of the dissolution capacity of fresh surface snow**, because CO2 diffusion through a thick, dense snowpack is limited (measured minimum CO2 concentration 0.005 vol-% at 300 cm snow depth, roughly quadrupling toward the surface) — i.e., **the system is CO2-starved while snow cover is thick and continuous**. This changes sharply once the snowpack thins in **July**, when the near-closed system opens to better atmospheric CO2 exchange, CO2 values rise, and — combined with increasing melt volumes — **maximum limestone dissolution (28 mg/L CaCO3) is reached late in the ablation season**, specifically as the snowpack is thinning rather than during peak snow depth or peak temperature.

*Relevance:* This is the direct mechanistic/quantitative basis for the thesis's proposed correlation variable — snow-cover *thinning date* rather than total snow duration or air temperature. It gives a specific, citable physical explanation (CO2 diffusion limitation under thick snow, opening up as snow thins) for why dissolution should track thinning-date rather than a simple degree-day or duration metric, from the same mountain range as the study site. Strongly recommend obtaining full-text access (institutional Schweizerbart subscription) to extract the precise dissolution-vs-date relationship/curve shape for direct comparison against the thesis's snow-cover remote-sensing data.

## 4. Frost weathering mechanics (karst-adjacent, not karst-specific) (Topic 3)

**Draebing, D. & Mayer, T. (2021): Topographic and geologic controls on frost cracking in Alpine rockwalls. *Journal of Geophysical Research: Earth Surface*, 126(6), e2021JF006163. DOI: 10.1029/2021JF006163.** **[full text read — open access, CC-BY, via Utrecht University repository copy]**

*Summary:* This is a rigorous, directly-measured (not just modelled) study of what controls frost-cracking intensity in alpine rockwalls, using rock-surface temperature loggers on six rockwalls (2580–3158 m, Swiss Alps) spanning opposing aspects and three lithologies (schisty quartz slate, amphibolite, aplite — **not limestone**, an important caveat for direct transfer to Wetterstein karst), combined with four different frost-cracking models (two purely thermal, two thermo-mechanical) validated against measured fracture spacing and Schmidt-hammer rock-strength data. Key findings directly transferable to a doline-wall context: (1) **snow cover has a dual, time-dependent thermal effect** — it warms the rock surface relative to air temperature for roughly the first half to two-thirds of the snow-cover period (insulation), then *cools* it relative to air temperature later in the snow-cover period (the thermal offset flips sign) — meaning snow's effect on rock temperature (and thus on frost-cracking windows) is not simply "insulating," it changes character over the season; (2) **aspect strongly controls the thermal regime independent of altitude** — south-facing rock surfaces were 2.2–6.0°C warmer (mean annual) than north-facing counterparts at the same/similar elevation, with north-facing walls hosting permafrost that south-facing walls at equivalent altitude lacked; (3) purely thermal frost-cracking models (temperature-gradient-only) and thermo-mechanical models (which incorporate rock strength, hydraulic permeability, and moisture availability) gave **contradictory spatial patterns**, and the thermo-mechanical models matched independently observed fracture spacing/rock-strength data better — the authors conclude water availability and rock mechanical properties, not just thermal cycling, are essential controls, and that assumed rock-moisture saturation (common in these models) is likely an *overestimate* on south-facing (drier) walls; (4) frost cracking intensity and rock strength did **not** always correlate as expected (e.g., the modelled-highest-frost-cracking wall had the *hardest* measured rock, contradicting the models) — a caution against assuming frost-cracking model output alone predicts observed rock condition.

*Relevance:* Not karst-specific and not doline-specific (no enclosed depressions, cold-air pools, or limestone examined), so this cannot be over-claimed as direct doline-wall evidence — a genuine, honestly-flagged gap: **I found no literature that measures frost weathering rates or mechanisms specifically on doline walls/enclosed karst depressions as opposed to open rockwalls.** That said, this is a strong, recent, methodologically rigorous baseline for (a) how to think about aspect-dependent frost weathering within a single doline (a doline has a full range of wall aspects in a small area, so this paper's north-vs-south asymmetry is directly applicable at doline scale), (b) the mechanistic argument for why snow's thermal effect on rock is time-varying rather than constant (relevant to linking frost-shatter timing to snow thinning date, paralleling the Küfmann CO2 argument), and (c) the caution that moisture/saturation assumptions dominate model uncertainty — likely also true for any attempt to model frost shatter around Zugspitzplatt dolines. Worth citing as the best available *mechanistic* frost-weathering reference while being explicit in the thesis that it is extrapolated from silicate rockwalls, not documented on limestone doline walls.

## 5. Repeat/multi-temporal survey precedents for quantifying doline or karst-depression change (Topic 4)

**Kim, J. & Hong, I. (2025): Analysis of doline microtopography in karst mountainous terrain using UAV LiDAR: a case study of 'Gulneomjae' in Mungyeong City, South Korea. *Sensors*, 25(14), 4350. DOI: 10.3390/s25144350.** **[full text/detailed content read via PMC open-access copy, PMC12297937]**

*Summary:* Methodologically close to what the thesis is doing, though **single-epoch, not multi-temporal** in its own right. UAV LiDAR (DJI Matrice 300 RTK + Zenmuse L2, ~98 points/m² raw density, 0.25 m DEM via TIN interpolation) mapped seven dolines in dense-vegetation karst terrain, substantially outperforming the national 1:5000 basemap DEM for boundary delineation and morphometry (area, perimeter, axes). The authors did compare their September 2024 survey against an ~11-year-old 2013 field/map baseline and found two previously-mapped dolines were no longer detectable, attributing this to "terrain changes, such as cultivation, erosion" — but they explicitly flag that the 11-year gap and heterogeneous data sources (map vs LiDAR) introduce large uncertainty, and that true change detection would require same-sensor repeat epochs.

*Relevance:* Useful as a UAV-LiDAR methodology precedent (sensor choice, point density, DEM resolution, doline delineation workflow) and — importantly — as an explicit cautionary example of exactly the pitfall the thesis must avoid: comparing genuinely different data sources/epochs risks mistaking sensor/resolution artefacts for real morphological change. Supports the thesis's use of matched repeat UAV/LiDAR epochs (2024–2026, same platform) as methodologically stronger than this precedent's before/after comparison.

**Securo, A., Del Gobbo, C. & Colucci, R. R. (2022): Multi-year evolution of 75 snow and ice deposits in schachtdolines and shafts of recently deglaciated karst terrain: observations from Mount Canin-Kanin, Julian Alps, Europe. *Geomorphology*, 417, 108434. DOI: 10.1016/j.geomorph.2022.108434.** **[abstract/summary only, via indexed search snippets — ScienceDirect, Wiley and SSRN copies all 403-blocked WebFetch; I could not verify the full argument first-hand]**

*Summary (as far as verifiable):* Reportedly analyses **six airborne LiDAR surveys spanning 2006–2018**, each flown at the end of the ablation season, over 75 permanent snow/ice deposits sitting inside schachtdolines (vertical/near-vertical karst shafts) and shafts on the Canin-Kanin massif (Julian Alps, recently deglaciated karst terrain), computing thickness and volume change per deposit and comparing against regional glacier mass balance. The repeated search-engine extracts consistently report a **general increase in deposit thickness (>1 m gain)** over the observed period, i.e., these doline/shaft-hosted snow-ice bodies behaved differently from — and apparently more favourably than — the surrounding glaciers.

*Relevance:* If the methodology summary is accurate, this is the strongest available precedent for exactly the kind of repeat-LiDAR, end-of-ablation-season, doline-scale volumetric change monitoring the thesis needs — same general approach (multi-epoch airborne/UAV LiDAR timed to ablation season, quantifying volume/thickness change in and around karst depressions) applied over a 12-year, six-epoch record rather than the thesis's shorter 2024–2026 window. Strongly recommend obtaining full-text access (Geomorphology, via institutional subscription) before citing methodology details, but this is worth prioritising — it is the closest topical and methodological analogue found in this search, from a nearby Alpine karst massif (Julian Alps) with a similar recently-deglaciated, high-alpine, doline-and-shaft-dominated setting to the Zugspitzplatt.

DOI: 10.1007/s11069-026-08275-7.** **[abstract/summary only, via indexed search snippets — Springer paywall blocked WebFetch; author names not recoverable via search]**

Low priority; flag as a methods lead only, and verify authorship/details via institutional access before citing.

## Ranked synthesis (most important first)

1. **Mix & Küfmann (2012), Zahmer Kaiser dolines** — the best direct analogue in the entire search: a population of century-old-mapped, common-origin dolines in the Northern Calcareous Alps shows *some* members actively growing (linked specifically to persistent snowpatches/meltwater) and others stagnant (linked to sediment infill), which is structurally the exact phenomenon the thesis is trying to explain at the Zugspitzplatt. Read this one in full via institutional access; it should anchor the thesis's Topic-1 argument and may offer a transferable field method (indicator vegetation for meltwater zones).

2. **Küfmann (2014, published; DOI dated 2013), rock/snow interface dissolution, Wetterstein Mountains** — almost certainly the actual paper behind the "Küfmann 2013" reference in the brief, from the very same mountain range. Gives the mechanistic, quantitative basis (CO2 diffusion-limitation under thick snow; dissolution peaking in July as the snowpack thins and opens to atmospheric CO2) for using snow-cover *thinning date* as the correlate rather than duration or temperature. Get full text to extract the exact dissolution-timing curve for direct comparison against the thesis's remote-sensing-derived thinning dates.

3. **Draebing & Mayer (2021), frost cracking in Alpine rockwalls** — not karst/doline-specific (an honest gap worth stating explicitly in the thesis), but the strongest available mechanistic frost-weathering reference: quantifies how aspect, snow-cover timing (not just presence/absence), rock type, and moisture assumptions control frost-cracking magnitude and depth. Directly useful for reasoning about aspect-dependent frost-shatter variability within single dolines (which expose a full range of wall aspects) and for the argument that snow's thermal effect on rock reverses sign partway through the snow-cover season.

4. **Securo, Del Gobbo & Colucci (2022), Mount Canin-Kanin snow/ice deposits in schachtdolines** — the closest methodological and geographic analogue for repeat-LiDAR doline-scale volumetric monitoring (six epochs, 2006–2018, end-of-ablation-season timing, nearby Julian Alps karst), but only verified via secondary search summaries — prioritise obtaining full text.

5. **Grüger & Jerz (2010)** — not new evidence of recent change, but the correct primary citation for the 7415 BP date, and useful for the "progressive karstification since formation" framing (the doline's own authors describe it as still accumulating and karstifying throughout the Holocene record).

6. **Veress (2017), glaciokarst doline development** — a potentially important synthesis on the self-reinforcing snow-accumulation/deepening feedback in Alpine/Dinaric dolines, but I could not verify its content beyond search-engine snippets; treat the "deepening and snow accumulation reinforce each other" claim as a hypothesis to check against the primary text, not a settled finding to cite outright.

7. **Kim & Hong (2025), Gulneomjae UAV LiDAR** — useful UAV-LiDAR methods precedent and a cautionary example of cross-epoch/cross-sensor comparison pitfalls, but single-epoch and not a true change-detection study.

8. **"Novel approach... multitemporal lidar" (Natural Hazards, 2026)** and the two Springer frost-hollow/cold-air-pooling book chapters — lowest priority; flagged only as leads for institutional-access follow-up, not verified in content.
