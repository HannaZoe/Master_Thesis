# Literature review: cold-climate, periglacial and permafrost-influenced karst

Compiled 2026-09-28 in support of the Zugspitzplatt doline thesis. Focus questions: (1) documented cold/polar/glaciokarst settings beyond the Alps; (2) whether permafrost presence/absence controls active vs relict dissolution, with direct (not just theoretical) evidence; (3) comparative/review literature on dissolution rates or doline activity across climate zones; (4) additional alpine glaciokarst plateaus comparable to the Zugspitzplatt, beyond Veress (2017) and Gams (2000).

Method note: sources below are only included where I actually retrieved and read real page/article content (abstract or full text), not just a search-engine snippet. Several promising papers were identified but blocked by paywalls/CAPTCHAs and could not be verified — these are listed separately at the end under "Identified but inaccessible" so the student can chase them down with institutional access; I have **not** attributed findings to them.

## 1. Salvigsen, O. & Elgersma, A. (1985). "Large-scale karst features and open taliks at Vardeborgsletta, outer Isfjorden, Svalbard." *Polar Research*, 3(2), 145–153. https://doi.org/10.3402/polar.v3i2.6948

**Summary:** Classic, direct field study of a limestone karst area (dolines, sinking streams, ponds with sinking drainage) at Vardeborgsletta on Svalbard, situated within the continuous permafrost zone. The authors show that large open taliks (unfrozen ground) occur locally, caused by the heat of deep-circulating groundwater. Where these taliks exist, groundwater can circulate and large-scale karst features have developed. The paper states explicitly that "karst processes are also active today, and dolines and ponds with sinking drainage occur" — i.e., contemporary, ongoing dissolution — but only in the talik-affected ground; the surrounding continuous permafrost is treated as the factor that otherwise blocks groundwater circulation and thus active karstification.

**Relevance:** This is about as close to a direct field test of the project's hypothesis as exists in the literature: in a high-Arctic, continuous-permafrost setting, karst dissolution is demonstrably ongoing wherever liquid water can circulate (via taliks), and is implicitly absent/blocked where the ground stays frozen year-round. It supports the "seasonal/local liquid water is necessary and sufficient" framing, while showing that "permafrost region" and "no dissolution" are not synonymous — the controlling variable is the local thermal/hydrological state (talik vs. frozen ground), not the regional climate classification.

## 2. Cohen, S. (2012). "A Karst Lake System in the High Arctic: A Case Study at Linnédalen, Svalbard." Undergraduate honours thesis, Mount Holyoke College. https://ida.mtholyoke.edu/items/579955eb-880b-4458-893c-236d15d27ed8

**Summary:** Investigates a karst lake system (limestone/dolomite/gypsum/anhydrite bedrock) in the Linnédalen valley, western Svalbard, sitting in continuous permafrost. The thesis explicitly frames and tests the older assumption that "only thermokarst features could form in the arctic, and true karst could not exist, because the chemical weathering needed to form karst was prevented by permafrost" — and argues against it, citing D.C. Ford's permafrost-karst model in which karstification is constrained to operate within the active layer, producing a genuine but shallow ("attenuated") karst rather than no karst at all. It draws on prior work (Firth River Basin, Canada) showing karst drainage moving through taliks and resurfacing as aufeis (icing), and notes that at Linnédalen the mechanism of vertical drainage below the ~2 m active layer was, at the time of writing, still not fully resolved.

**Relevance:** Directly useful as a secondary confirmation of Ford's theoretical model (karst restricted to the active layer/taliks under permafrost, not eliminated) and as evidence that the "permafrost = no karst" assumption is outdated in the literature. Being a student thesis rather than a peer-reviewed paper, it should be cited cautiously (better to chase the primary Ford source it cites if possible), but its synthesis of the debate is a good calibration point for how the field's thinking evolved from "karst impossible under permafrost" toward "karst confined to seasonally-thawed zones."

## 3. Ford, D. (2009). "Mapping Known and Potential Karst Areas in the Northwest Territories, Canada." Report prepared for Environment and Natural Resources, Government of the Northwest Territories.

**Summary:** A government geoscience report by one of the foremost authorities on cold-climate karst, surveying karst across the NWT (Nahanni, Ram Plateau, Horton River, Bear Rock, etc.), most of which lies in discontinuous-to-continuous permafrost. Central, repeatedly stated finding: permafrost inhibits groundwater flow through the upper tens of metres of rock and thereby suppresses active karstification; in areas of continuous permafrost (e.g. Parry Peninsula, Campbell Uplift) karst is described as "largely arrested by permafrost today." Timing matters too — on the Ram Plateau, karst is muted because the plateau was tectonically uplifted into karstifiable position only after permafrost had already established itself, so karst never got a real foothold before freezing shut. Notably, the report also documents an apparent exception: in the Horton River area, active sinking streams persist even in dolomite (a less-soluble rock) under deep permafrost and a cold, dry climate — Ford treats this as anomalous/surprising rather than as disproof of the general pattern, implying it depends on unusually strong hydraulic gradients concentrating flow (and presumably seasonal/talik-related liquid water) despite the harsh regional climate.

**Relevance:** This is the strongest single piece of evidence for point 2 of the task. It gives a direct, regional-scale, multi-site assessment (not a single locality) stating in plain terms that continuous permafrost arrests active karst development, that karst reactivates/persists where taliks or sufficient hydraulic gradient exist, and that the timing of permafrost onset relative to karst inception matters (directly relevant to reasoning about a "mixed-age" doline population like the Zugspitzplatt's). The Horton River exception is a useful caution against treating "permafrost inhibits dissolution" as an absolute rule — the project's hypothesis should probably be phrased in terms of *local* liquid-water availability (taliks, springs, high hydraulic gradient) rather than regional permafrost extent alone.

## 4. Mix, C. & Küfmann, C. (2012). "Genesis and controlling factors of dolines in subalpine karst, Northern Calcareous Alps (Plateau Zahmer Kaiser, Austria)." *Zeitschrift für Geomorphologie*, 56(2), 141–163. https://doi.org/10.1127/0372-8854/2011/0065

**Summary:** A morphometric re-survey of dolines on the Zahmer Kaiser plateau (Kaiser Mountains, Tyrol), comparing current doline morphology against the first field mapping from 1911. The plateau is a subalpine karst surface in the Northern Calcareous Alps — geologically and climatically a very close analogue to the Zugspitzplatt. The authors find two contrasting doline development trajectories operating side by side on the same plateau: (a) "progressive transformation" — continued growth in diameter, depth and volume, driven by meltwater from long-lasting snowpatches sitting in the doline hollows (i.e., seasonal liquid water from snowmelt is the active dissolution agent); and (b) "regressive/stagnating" development, where dolines stop deepening because of infilling by soil, aeolian dust and alpine loess, which blocks further corrosive water contact with bedrock. Both trajectories are shown to be governed by microtopography and the doline's immediate surroundings (which control local snow distribution and sediment supply), rather than by a single regional climatic signal.

**Relevance:** This is an excellent close analogue for the Zugspitzplatt beyond Veress and Gams: same mountain range type (Northern Calcareous Alps), same subalpine/periglacial-belt setting, same rock type context, and — crucially — it demonstrates with direct re-survey evidence (not modelling) that individual dolines on one plateau can be simultaneously active (still deepening via snowmelt-driven dissolution) or effectively dormant/stagnant (sealed by sediment infill), independent of glaciation history. This is a strong, literature-grounded precedent for the thesis's "mixed-age/mixed-activity population" framing, and it reinforces that seasonal snowmelt (liquid water), not ambient air temperature, is the operative dissolution driver even in a periglacial-belt plateau setting.

## 5. Bartolomé, M., Cazenave, G., Luetscher, M., Spötl, C., Gázquez, F., Belmonte, Á., Turchyn, A. V., López-Moreno, J. I., and Moreno, A. (2023). "Mountain permafrost in the Central Pyrenees: insights from the Devaux ice cave." *The Cryosphere*, 17, 477–497. https://doi.org/10.5194/tc-17-477-2023

**Summary:** Open-access study of an ice-bearing cave sitting within discontinuous mountain permafrost in the Central Pyrenees (permafrost there occurs above ~2600–3000 m). The cave has two sectors with different thermal regimes: well-ventilated entrance galleries, versus isolated inner chambers where air and rock temperature stay below 0 °C year-round. In the permanently-frozen inner chambers the authors report the "absence of drips and/or seepage" — i.e., no liquid water reaching the ice/rock surfaces — and correspondingly find that older gypsum deposits have been preserved rather than dissolved, precisely because the persistently dry, frozen environment prevents further dissolution. Ice volume in these permafrost-sealed chambers changes by only ~0.6–2.2 mm/yr, which the authors explicitly contrast with "rapid changes observed in ice caves outside permafrost areas."

**Relevance:** This is a near-perfect process analogue for the Zugspitzplatt's own ice cave/tunnel discovery (massive ground ice found during 1985 tunnel construction at 2570 m, ~–1.5 °C, within the karst). It provides a second, independent, directly-measured (not just inferred) case of a European alpine karst-permafrost system where dissolution is measurably suppressed under permanently frozen, dry conditions, and where the contrast with non-permafrost ice caves is quantified. This strongly supports treating persistent ground ice/permafrost occurrences within the Zugspitzplatt karst as zones of currently-inhibited (dormant) dissolution, distinct from dolines with active seasonal meltwater.

## 6. Hagg, W. & Mayer, C. (2026). "From Snowferner to no ferner: The deglaciation of Zugspitzplatt." *Annals of Glaciology*, 67, e9, 1–6. https://doi.org/10.1017/aog.2026.10042 (open access)

**Summary:** Primarily a glaciological mass-balance reconstruction (1892–2023) of the Nördlicher and Südlicher Schneeferner on the Zugspitzplatt, showing near-total ice loss (52 Mt in 1892 to <1 Mt by 2023) and projecting complete disappearance around 2030. Not centrally about dolines or permafrost, but directly and usefully confirms current site conditions: the plateau is described as "an undulated but relatively low-sloping, tertiary-aged paleo-surface... This karst plateau covers an area of about 6 km²" between ~2000–2700 m a.s.l. On post-glacial hydrology, the authors note the bedrock overdeepening at Nördlicher Schneeferner could in principle be filled by a ~16 m deep tarn, but conclude this is unlikely because "the heavily karstified Wetterstein limestone preferably drains underground" — i.e., the plateau's karst conduit system is presently open and hydrologically active (transmitting water), not sealed by ice or sediment.

**Relevance:** Useful, verified, current (2026) confirmation from specialists actively working the Zugspitzplatt that (a) its karst drainage system is functioning today (water sinks underground rather than ponding), which is a precondition for any argument that dissolution could still be occurring somewhere in the system, and (b) the plateau's own glacier ice is now essentially gone, sharpening the contrast between the disappearing surface ice and any relict ground ice/permafrost that may persist in doline floors or subsurface karst — exactly the ambiguity the thesis is trying to resolve doline-by-doline.

## 7. Munich Alpine Hazards and Mitigation Cluster (AlpHaz), TUM — project page "Permafrost at the Zugspitze." https://www.cee.ed.tum.de/alphaz/research/projects/article/permafrost-at-the-zugspitze/

**Summary:** Not a peer-reviewed paper but a research-group project summary (TU Munich, Krautblatter's group) describing ongoing electrical-resistivity-tomography and borehole monitoring of a relict permafrost lens near the Zugspitze summit (in a ~300 m tunnel at 2800 m), with core temperatures of –0.5 to –1.5 °C and a highly patchy distribution restricted to steep, north-exposed rock. It records a ~1 °C rise in annual mean air temperature over the 20th century (–5.0 °C in 1901–1930 to –3.9 °C in 1991–2007) alongside a "highly sensitive response" of the permafrost body to that warming. Importantly, the page states that "karst vessels [conduits] in the rock have a strong influence on the distribution of permafrost" at the site.

**Relevance:** This is the clearest statement found linking karst structure and permafrost distribution specifically at the Zugspitze/Zugspitzplatt massif — i.e., the karst conduit network itself is shaping where ground ice does or doesn't persist (likely via air/water circulation through open conduits ventilating some areas while sealed pockets stay frozen). This is a mechanistic bridge directly relevant to interpreting why some Zugspitzplatt dolines might retain relict ice while others do not, and it corroborates that the massif's permafrost is "sensitive" and presumably degrading — consistent with a scenario of progressive reactivation of dissolution in previously-frozen doline floors.

## Ranked synthesis (most important first)

1. **Salvigsen & Elgersma (1985)** and **Ford (2009, NWT report)** together give the strongest, most direct field-based answer to the core question: in continuous permafrost, karst dissolution is not simply "slow" — it is described in the literature as **arrested/inhibited** wherever the ground stays frozen year-round, and becomes **active again specifically where liquid water is available**, whether via open taliks (Svalbard) or locally strong hydraulic gradients (Horton River, NWT). Both sources treat permafrost as controlling *whether* dissolution can happen at all in a given patch of ground, not merely how fast it happens.

2. **Bartolomé et al. (2023, Devaux ice cave, Pyrenees)** is the best process-level confirmation, with actual measured rates: dissolution/deposition is essentially stopped (no drips, gypsum preserved rather than dissolved, near-static ice volumes of ~1–2 mm/yr) in permanently sub-zero cave chambers, versus much more dynamic behaviour in non-permafrost ice caves. This is a close analogue to the Zugspitzplatt's own relict-ice tunnel discovery and directly supports treating ice-filled dolines/conduits as currently dormant.

3. **Mix & Küfmann (2012, Zahmer Kaiser)** is the best morphological/comparative analogue beyond Veress and Gams: on one Northern Calcareous Alps subalpine plateau, individual dolines simultaneously show active snowmelt-driven deepening and dormant/stagnant, sediment-sealed states — an almost direct template for arguing a mixed-activity doline population on the Zugspitzplatt, and independent evidence that seasonal meltwater (not regional climate alone) is the operative dissolution trigger even above the treeline in a periglacial-belt setting.

4. **Cohen (2012, Linnédalen thesis)**, citing Ford's permafrost-karst model, is useful mainly for articulating the theoretical mechanism cleanly: under permafrost, karstification does not switch off entirely but is confined to the active layer and to taliks, producing a shallow/attenuated karst rather than none at all. Treat as a secondary source pointing to Ford's primary work (chase Ford & Williams 2007 ch. 10, or Ford's original Canadian karst-cold-climate papers, for a citable primary version of this argument).

5. **The Zugspitzplatt-specific sources (Hagg & Mayer 2026; AlpHaz/TUM project page)** don't answer the permafrost-dissolution question on their own but establish current site facts the thesis needs: the plateau's karst conduits are hydrologically open and actively draining today, its own glacier ice is nearly gone, its summit-area permafrost is patchy, thin, and explicitly shaped by the karst conduit network itself, and that permafrost is described as "highly sensitive" to the ~1 °C warming already recorded — consistent with a plateau in which some formerly ice-sealed dolines could plausibly be re-activating now.

6. **Zerga (2024) systematic review** contributes only a single calibration data point (cold + polar climates together host over a third of global carbonate/karst terrain by area), underlining that cold-climate karst is a large, mainstream topic, not a fringe case — but its promised comparative discussion of rates by climate zone could not be verified and should be retrieved directly if needed.

### Direct answer to the permafrost/dissolution question

The literature actually retrieved and read for this review **confirms and sharpens** the project's existing understanding, rather than simply validating it at face value:

- **Confirmed:** every directly-evidenced source found (Salvigsen & Elgersma 1985; Ford 2009; Bartolomé et al. 2023) treats continuous, year-round-frozen ground as a state in which carbonate/evaporite dissolution is inhibited or effectively stopped, and treats the appearance of liquid water — whether from taliks, seasonal snowmelt, or hydraulic-gradient-driven groundwater flow — as the condition that switches dissolution back on. None of these sources found evidence of dissolution proceeding through solid, undisturbed permafrost/ice itself.
- **Refinement 1 — it's local, not regional:** the controlling variable in the literature is not "is this region classified as permafrost climate" but "is *this specific patch of rock/sediment* currently thawed, seasonally thawed, or hydraulically connected to a talik/spring." Both Svalbard sites and the NWT report show active karst persisting as isolated pockets inside otherwise continuously-frozen terrain. For the Zugspitzplatt this means doline-by-doline (or even within-doline) variation in activity is exactly what the literature would predict, not an anomaly requiring special explanation.
- **Refinement 2 — permafrost can be locally overridden by strong flow:** the Horton River exception in Ford (2009) shows that sufficiently strong hydraulic gradients can sustain active karst even through deep, cold permafrost in a dry climate, in the least-soluble rock type discussed. This is a caution against an absolute "permafrost = zero dissolution" rule; it should be framed as "permafrost strongly inhibits dissolution unless a persistent liquid-water pathway (talik, spring, strong gradient) exists," which is functionally the same as the project's "needs seasonal/liquid water" framing but stated at finer spatial resolution.
- **Refinement 3 — dissolution and permafrost can coexist in time, in a mosaic:** Mix & Küfmann (2012) show that on a single alpine karst plateau closely analogous to the Zugspitzplatt, some dolines are presently still deepening via snowmelt while immediately adjacent ones have stagnated — reinforcing that the Zugspitzplatt's population being a mix of a directly-dated 7415 BP doline and undated others is entirely consistent with, and even expected from, how these systems behave elsewhere in the Alps.

In short: the seasonal-liquid-water requirement for dissolution holds up well against the literature, but the better-supported version of it is spatially finer-grained than "cold climate vs. not" — it is about whether an individual doline, conduit, or ice body currently has (or has recently regained, via permafrost degradation) a liquid-water pathway, regardless of the surrounding region's overall climate classification.
