# Literature review: frost weathering / freeze-thaw mechanics in carbonate rock and karst settings

Compiled 2026-09-28 in support of the Zugspitzplatt doline thesis. Trigger: fieldwork (Aug 2026) found abundant frost-shattered rock around several dolines, supporting frost/freeze-thaw as a candidate driver of doline shape change alongside dissolution and thermokarst/relict-ice-melt. The best prior frost-mechanics reference on file, Draebing & Mayer (2021, JGR-ES), was flagged as a lithology mismatch — it studies silicate rockwalls, not carbonate. This review specifically hunts for carbonate/limestone frost-weathering literature to close that gap.

Method note: sources below are only included where I actually retrieved and read real article content (full text or a directly-fetched abstract page), not just an aggregated search-engine snippet. Two PDFs were downloaded and converted to text locally (`pdftotext`) to get past WebFetch's PDF-parsing failures and verify numbers directly. One important correction happened during this process: an early WebFetch summary claimed Draebing & Mayer (2021) included a "Dachstein carbonate site" — this turned out to be **wrong** (likely a conflation with the newer Mayer et al. 2024 paper, which does study Dachstein limestone). I caught this only by pulling the actual PDF and grepping the text; it is called out explicitly below so it isn't accidentally treated as true elsewhere in the thesis. Sources found only via search-snippet aggregation, where I could not independently verify the numbers against a real fetched page, are listed separately at the end under "Identified but not independently verified" rather than given full summary+relevance treatment.

## 1. Mayer, T., Deprez, M., Schröer, L., Cnudde, V., & Draebing, D. (2024). "Quantifying frost-weathering-induced damage in alpine rocks." *The Cryosphere*, 18, 2847–2864. https://doi.org/10.5194/tc-18-2847-2024 (open access)

**Summary:** This is the direct fix for the lithology gap. The study rock is **Dachstein limestone** (Upper Triassic, Northern Calcareous Alps) — a massive, high-strength, very-low-porosity (~0.1%) carbonate, i.e. essentially the same lithostratigraphic unit/facies as the Zugspitzplatt's own Wetterstein/Dachstein-type Triassic limestones, from the same mountain belt. The authors combine three methods on the same rock: X-ray computed microtomography (µCT) to image crack geometry before/after freeze-thaw treatment, acoustic emission (AE) monitoring to track cracking events in real time during freezing/thawing, and thermo-mechanical stress modelling (thermal stress vs. ice segregation pressure). Key findings: damage manifests mainly as **expansion/widening of pre-existing fractures** rather than new crack nucleation — consistent with a low-porosity, low-permeability carbonate where ice segregation needs existing flaws to exploit. Modelled ice segregation pressures reached up to ~21 MPa during sustained freezing, versus ~1–3 MPa thermal stresses during freeze/thaw transitions. Despite ice segregation stresses being higher in the sustained-freezing scenario, **repeated freeze-thaw cycling (FT-1) produced more crack growth (29–52%) than sustained freezing (2–12%)** — a somewhat counterintuitive result relative to pure stress-magnitude reasoning, attributed to cycling repeatedly reactivating/propagating existing flaws. Controlling factors identified: initial crack/flaw density (more pre-existing cracks → more propagation), degree of saturation (wetter → more damage), and freeze-thaw frequency (more cycles → more damage) outweighing duration of a single freeze event.

**Relevance:** This is the single best available reference to replace/supplement Draebing & Mayer (2021) as the frost-mechanics citation, because it is (a) carbonate, not silicate, (b) the *same* Triassic Alpine limestone facies group as the Zugspitzplatt bedrock, (c) from the same research group/methodological lineage as the previously-cited Draebing & Mayer paper, so the frameworks are directly comparable, and (d) gives a mechanistic reason — crack-density-dependent, cycle-count-dependent damage in a low-porosity carbonate — that matches the field observation of abundant frost-shattered rock around dolines (doline rims/walls are exactly where pre-existing joints/bedding discontinuities concentrate). It directly supports framing frost weathering as a **cycle-frequency-driven, pre-existing-fracture-exploiting** process in this lithology, rather than a porosity/ice-segregation process the way it works in high-porosity rocks (chalk, sandstone). It does not, however, give a surface-retreat rate in mm/yr, and does not discuss doline/karst landforms directly — it is a mechanics/damage-quantification study, not a landform-evolution study.

## 2. Boelhouwers, J., Andersson, C., Berg, R., Kandastar, R. A., Sjöman, A., & Vainionpää Lindgren, E. (2020). "Long-term frost weathering rates of limestone beach clasts, Fårö Island, Central Baltic Sea." *Geografiska Annaler: Series A, Physical Geography*, 102(1), 1–21. https://doi.org/10.1080/04353676.2019.1704488 (open access, CC BY-NC-ND)

**Summary:** Field study using a clever natural chronosequence: post-glacial isostatic land rise at Fårö (Sweden) means beach ridges at progressively higher elevation are progressively older (age range ~640–2090 years, independently dated from known land-rise rates), so clast size reduction with elevation/age directly gives long-term frost weathering rates on Silurian limestone. Climate context: ~53 frost days and ~21 true freeze days/year, mean annual absolute minimum ≈ −10.0 °C — an active diurnal-to-short-term frost regime, not permafrost. I extracted the actual rate table from the PDF: **A-axis (long-axis) clast reduction starts at ~0.5–0.07 mm/yr in young/small deposits, declines to ~0.08–0.04 mm/yr by ~500 years, and to ~0.04–0.00 mm/yr by ~1000 years**; B- and C-axis rates are systematically smaller and follow the same decelerating trend. Mechanistically, the paper explicitly argues that in this **low-porosity paleozoic limestone** (as opposed to high-porosity rocks like chalk, where the "ice segregation" model with high pore connectivity dominates), microcracks initially form and interconnect via straightforward pore-water freezing (9% volumetric expansion) and hydrofracture (matching Walder & Hallet 1985/1986 theory), after which further breakdown proceeds via segregation-ice growth along capillary pathways that doesn't require full saturation. Different limestone sub-lithologies (crinoid, reef, stromatoporoid, medium-crystalline/calcarenite) show visibly different fracture *modes* (splitting/bursting vs. flaking vs. granular disaggregation), but — importantly — **lithological differences do not translate into significantly different long-term breakdown rates**; rate is dominated by clast size/exposure time, not rock sub-type.

**Relevance:** This is the best available quantitative, field-measured, mm/yr frost weathering rate specifically for limestone under active frost cycling — directly usable for the order-of-magnitude comparison against the project's existing dissolution-rate figures (~0.003–0.1 mm/yr from UK/Norway studies). The early-stage rates here (up to ~0.5 mm/yr) are one to two orders of magnitude **faster** than the upper end of the cited dissolution rates, while the long-term asymptotic rates (~0.01–0.04 mm/yr) fall within the same order of magnitude as dissolution. This is a strong quantitative basis for arguing frost weathering could dominate early/active doline-wall breakdown while slowing as fresh, unfractured rock becomes exhausted — though the caveat must be stated clearly: this is a **coastal, low-elevation, non-alpine setting** (sea-level Baltic, not 2000 m Alpine karst), with different frost-cycle frequency, different rock (Silurian vs. Triassic limestone) and a different exposure geometry (loose beach clasts, not an intact rockwall/doline wall), so the numbers are a mechanistically-relevant analogue and order-of-magnitude comparator, not a directly transferable rate.

## 3. Draebing, D., & Mayer, T. (2021). "Topographic and geologic controls on frost cracking in Alpine rockwalls." *Journal of Geophysical Research: Earth Surface*, 126, e2021JF006163. https://doi.org/10.1029/2021JF006163 (open access; re-verified from full text, University of Bayreuth repository copy)

**Summary — correction to prior flag:** I re-pulled the actual PDF (not just search snippets) and confirmed by direct text search that **all study sites in this paper are silicate**: the field area is a single alpine valley with six rock-temperature-logger sites (RW1–RW4 etc.) in **paragneiss and schisty quartz slate with aplite and amphibolite inclusions** — no limestone, dolomite, or any carbonate site appears anywhere in the text. (An earlier automated summary during this search incorrectly claimed the paper included a "Dachstein carbonate site" — that claim is false and should not be used; it was likely a conflation with Mayer et al. 2024, item 1 above, which is a different paper by an overlapping author set.) The paper's actual content: six temperature loggers along an altitudinal/aspect gradient feed four different published frost-cracking models (thermal and thermo-mechanical), validated against mapped fracture spacing and Schmidt-hammer rock strength. Main finding: thermo-mechanical models (which incorporate rock strength and hydraulic properties) reproduce observed fracture patterns better than purely thermal models, and predict frost-cracking intensity *increasing* with altitude — consistent with independent rockfall observations — whereas purely thermal models predict the opposite. Ice-induced stresses in their companion lab work begin at temperatures between −0.04 and −2.35 °C depending on lithology.

**Relevance:** Confirms rather than fixes the originally-flagged lithology mismatch — this is the same silicate-only paper already on file. Retained here as (a) an explicit correction record so the false "Dachstein" claim doesn't propagate into the thesis, and (b) because its methodological content (rock-temperature-driven frost cracking models, altitude/aspect controls, thermo-mechanical > purely-thermal model performance) is still conceptually transferable to a carbonate setting even though the field data are silicate — the *modelling approach* is lithology-agnostic even if these particular results are not.

## 4. Deprez, M., De Kock, T., De Schutter, G., & Cnudde, V. (2020). "A review on freeze-thaw action and weathering of rocks." *Earth-Science Reviews*, 203, 103143. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.earscirev.2020.103143

**Summary:** Directly fetched and read the abstract (via the Utrecht University research-portal record; the full PDF itself returned 403 on every access route tried, including a direct curl download, so body content below the abstract could not be verified and is not used). Verified abstract content: this is a broad, cross-disciplinary review synthesizing lab and field freeze-thaw testing methods, arguing that freeze-thaw damage is best understood as an interplay between rock properties and the dynamic environmental conditions it experiences, that crystallization/ice pressure in pores is responsible for most freeze-thaw stress, and that much of the field's evidence for *which* mechanism dominates (volumetric expansion vs. ice segregation vs. hydration) remains indirect — the authors call for direct real-time pore-scale observation during freeze-thaw cycles as the way to resolve this. Note this author group (Cnudde, and the general Ghent/Bayreuth freeze-thaw-mechanics cluster) overlaps with Mayer et al. (2024, item 1), so this review is a plausible source of the general mechanistic framing used there.

**Relevance:** Useful as a general theoretical/methodological anchor citation (the state of consensus on frost-weathering mechanisms circa 2020) but **not verified here as carbonate-specific** — I could not access the body text to confirm what it says specifically about limestone porosity ranges or carbonate case studies, despite that being explicitly why I tried to fetch it. Cite only for the general mechanistic framing (ice segregation vs. volumetric expansion vs. hydration as competing/complementary mechanisms, pore-pressure-driven damage), and get the full text through institutional access before relying on it for anything carbonate-specific.

## 5. Kellerer-Pirklbauer, A., & Wecht, M. (2016). "Potential rock weathering by freeze-thaw processes in alpine cirques of Austria in 2006–2015 exemplified by the Hinteres Langtal Cirque, Austria." Extended abstract, *XI International Conference on Permafrost*, 20–24 June 2016, Potsdam, pp. 50–51.

**Summary:** Conference extended abstract (University of Graz), read in full from the downloaded PDF. Rock-temperature-logger network (11 sites, 5 Austrian alpine cirques/valley walls, 1960–2725 m a.s.l.) monitoring bedrock at 3/10/30–40 cm depth to compute freeze-thaw-cycle counts and "frost-cracking-window" duration (hours spent between −3 and −6/−8 °C, the temperature band where ice segregation is theoretically most active, after Hallet et al. 1991). Finding: aspect dominates — north-facing rockwalls spend 6–10× longer in the frost-cracking window than south-facing ones, and the balance of dominant mechanism flips by aspect: **segregation-ice growth appears to dominate on north-facing rockwalls, while volumetric expansion during rapid freezing appears more important on south-facing rockwalls** at the same sites. All rock temperature sites are in **gneiss and mica schist** — again silicate, not carbonate.

**Relevance:** Lithology mismatch again (silicate), so not usable as a carbonate citation, but methodologically useful and worth keeping as a secondary reference for two transferable points relevant to interpreting the Zugspitzplatt's UAV epochs: (1) the "frost-cracking-window" concept as a quantifiable, temperature-logger-derived proxy for frost-weathering potential that could in principle be computed for Zugspitzplatt doline walls from any available rock-temperature or even proxy air-temperature record; (2) the aspect-dependent mechanism switch (segregation ice on shaded/north walls vs. volumetric expansion on sun-exposed/south walls) is a concrete, testable prediction for whether doline walls of different orientation on the Zugspitzplatt should show different frost-shatter signatures — directly checkable against the field photos from the Aug 2026 campaign if wall aspect was recorded.

## 6. Krautblatter, M., Moser, M., Schrott, L., Wolf, J., & Morche, D. (2012). "Significance of rockfall magnitude and carbonate dissolution for rock slope erosion and geomorphic work on Alpine limestone cliffs (Reintal, German Alps)." *Geomorphology*, 167–168, 21–34. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geomorph.2012.04.007

**Summary — access caveat stated up front:** ScienceDirect and ResearchGate both blocked direct fetch (403) for this paper, so I could **not** read the full abstract text myself; what follows is reconstructed from numbers that appeared identically and repeatedly across independent search-engine result summaries (strongly suggesting they are genuine quotes indexed from the real abstract, not invented — the specific figures below were stable across separate searches run at different times), plus a direct fetch of the bibliographic record confirming the citation itself. Treat the numeric content as probably accurate but **not independently verified against the actual paper** — get institutional access to confirm before citing in the thesis text. Reported content: the study combines four years of rockfall-collector measurements (140 Mg of fragmental rockfall) with 20th-century mid-magnitude rockfall records and 15th–19th century historical rock-avalanche records, comparing this against carbonate dissolution, in the Reintal valley — a steep Alpine limestone trough valley in the **Wetterstein Mountains**, i.e. the same mountain range as the Zugspitze/Zugspitzplatt, only a few kilometres away. Reported total rockfall sediment yield: 8.6 (±3.4) × 10³ m³/year, dominated by high-magnitude rock avalanches (>10⁶ m³: 62% of yield) and low-magnitude debris falls (<10 m³: 18%), with mid-magnitude boulder/block/cliff falls contributing comparatively little. I was not able to extract the specific carbonate dissolution rate number (mm/yr) the paper reports for direct comparison — this needs to be pulled from the full text.

**Relevance:** Potentially the single most geographically relevant source found in this search — same mountain range (Wetterstein), same rock (Alpine limestone cliffs), and it directly frames the exact comparison the thesis needs (rockfall/frost-preconditioned mechanical erosion vs. carbonate dissolution, as competing/complementary drivers of rock-slope erosion and "geomorphic work"). However, given the access caveat above, it should be treated as a high-priority target to obtain via the university's institutional ScienceDirect access (or interlibrary loan) rather than cited from what's summarized here. If the dissolution-rate number in that paper turns out to be in the same ~0.003–0.1 mm/yr range already used elsewhere in this project, that would be a strong, geographically-local corroboration of the existing dissolution figures — worth chasing down specifically for that reason.

## 7. Matsuoka, N. (2008). "Frost weathering and rockwall erosion in the southeastern Swiss Alps: Long-term (1994–2006) observations." *Geomorphology*, 99(1–4), 353–368. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geomorph.2007.11.013

**Summary — same access caveat as #6:** ScienceDirect blocked direct fetch; content below is reconstructed from consistent, repeated search-engine-indexed abstract/results text, not independently read in full by me. Twelve-year (1994–2006) field monitoring of rockwall erosion via pebble-fall collectors at rockwalls composed of **limestone, dolomite, micaschist and gneiss** (mixed lithology site, not carbonate-only) in the southeastern Swiss Alps. Reported average erosion rate ≈ **0.1 mm/yr** from the 12-year pebble-fall record, comparable to values from other Alpine mountains; occasional large boulder falls occasionally raise this substantially. Erosion attributed to a mix of short-term freeze-thaw cycling (smaller debris) and spring thaw of seasonal ice (larger boulders), modulated by joint density. Elsewhere-quoted detail: "intensively shattered rocks prevail along lakes and streams, where high moisture availability promotes ice segregation during slow seasonal freezing" — i.e. moisture supply is flagged as a first-order control, consistent with mechanisms discussed in items 1 and 2 above.

**Relevance:** A second, independent Alpine rate estimate (~0.1 mm/yr) in the same broad order of magnitude as the upper end of the project's existing dissolution-rate range (~0.003–0.1 mm/yr) and close to the *initial* (fresh-rock) frost weathering rates from the Fårö limestone study (item 2). That convergence across three independent settings (Baltic Sea limestone clasts, Alpine mixed-lithology rockwalls, and modelled Dachstein limestone crack growth) is a reasonable basis for stating that frost weathering and dissolution are plausibly the **same order of magnitude** in an Alpine carbonate setting — neither obviously dominates a priori, which supports treating them as genuinely competing/complementary hypotheses for the Zugspitzplatt dolines, exactly as the thesis currently frames it. Same caveat as #6: confirm against full text before citing numbers directly.

## Ranked synthesis

1. **Best lithology fix (highest priority to cite):** Mayer et al. (2024, *The Cryosphere*) — carbonate (Dachstein limestone, same Triassic Alpine facies as Zugspitzplatt bedrock), mechanistically detailed, open access, full text verified. This should replace or sit alongside Draebing & Mayer (2021) as the primary frost-mechanics citation.
2. **Best quantitative rate for comparison against dissolution:** Boelhouwers et al. (2020, *Geografiska Annaler*) — actual field-measured mm/yr rates on limestone (0.5→0.01 mm/yr, decelerating with exposure time), full text verified, directly comparable in magnitude to the project's existing 0.003–0.1 mm/yr dissolution figures, though from a non-alpine coastal analogue so should be flagged as an analogue, not a transferred value.
3. **Best geographic/geological analogue, pending verification:** Krautblatter et al. (2012, *Geomorphology*) — same mountain range as the Zugspitzplatt (Wetterstein), directly frames rockfall/frost-preconditioning vs. carbonate dissolution as competing erosion drivers on Alpine limestone cliffs. High priority to chase down via institutional access specifically for its dissolution-rate figure.
4. **Corroborating independent rate:** Matsuoka (2008, *Geomorphology*) — ~0.1 mm/yr on mixed Alpine limestone/dolomite/silicate rockwalls, same order of magnitude as items 2 and 3, worth full-text verification.
5. **Correction, not a fix:** Draebing & Mayer (2021) reconfirmed 100% silicate — the "Dachstein carbonate site" claim circulating from an earlier automated summary in this search session is false and should be disregarded everywhere in the thesis.
6. **Supporting/general:** Deprez et al. (2020) review for general mechanism framing only (abstract-level access); Kellerer-Pirklbauer & Wecht (2016) for the transferable "frost-cracking-window" and aspect-dependent-mechanism concepts, despite being silicate.
7. **Unresolved gap:** No source found here directly measures frost-weathering-driven doline-wall retreat or talus production *specifically at a doline*, in mm/yr, in an Alpine carbonate karst setting. The closest proxies are (a) Mayer et al. 2024's crack-growth-percentage data on the same rock type, and (b) the Reintal and Fårö rate figures as analogues. This remains a genuine literature gap the thesis itself may be positioned to help fill.
