### Compiled for the Zugspitzplatt collapse-doline thesis (karst dissolution vs. thermokarst causation)

## 1. Gude, M. & Barsch, D. (2005)

**Citation:** Gude, M. & Barsch, D. (2005). Assessment of geomorphic hazards in connection with permafrost occurrence in the Zugspitze area (Bavarian Alps, Germany). *Geomorphology*, 66(1–4), 85–93. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geomorph.2004.03.013

**Verification status:** Bibliographic details confirmed (DOI resolves to this article; journal/volume/pages cross-confirmed via TUM AlpHaz project page reference list and independent search aggregation). Full text NOT accessed (ScienceDirect abstract page and DOI redirect both returned 403 Forbidden on repeated attempts). Content summary below is reconstructed from converging secondary descriptions, not primary reading — treat with appropriate caution, though the specific factual details (year, depth, temperature, tunnel) recur identically across multiple independent aggregations, which is reassuring but not equivalent to verification.

**Summary (as reconstructed):** During 1985 construction of the Bavarian Zugspitzbahn rack-railway tunnel toward the Zugspitzplatt, massive ground ice was unexpectedly encountered across the full tunnel cross-section over a ~19 m stretch, at roughly 2570 m elevation, with ice temperatures around −1.5 °C. This ice body lies within/beneath a suspected collapse doline on the plateau. The paper frames this as *relict* ground ice (not seasonal), and states that thawing of this relict ice has *repeatedly* caused subsidence at this and other locations on the Zugspitzplatt, requiring remedial stabilization (elsewhere reported, including by the thesis's own framing, as concrete injection). The paper's broader argument is that permafrost — present at the Zugspitze summit and in isolated relict patches at lower/plateau elevations — poses a geomorphic hazard to tourist infrastructure (cable car stations, ski-lift masts, the rack-railway tunnel) via degradation-driven subsidence, based on BTS (basal temperature of snow) surveys.

**Relevance to Zugspitzplatt causation question:** This is the anchor precedent already given in the thesis brief. It is the single clearest documented case anywhere of ice-melt-driven subsidence occurring *inside a doline* on the Zugspitzplatt itself, tied to a specific, dated, engineering-verified event (not inference from morphology). It directly supports a thermokarst contribution to at least some doline subsidence on this plateau, but it is a *local* case (one tunnel intersection, one ice body) and does not by itself establish that other, non-tunnel-intersected dolines on the plateau share the same cause — the thesis's core problem (karst vs. thermokarst vs. both) remains open beyond this one instance.

## 2. Olefeldt, D. et al. (2016)

**Citation:** Olefeldt, D., Goswami, S., Grosse, G., Hayes, D., Hugelius, G., Kuhry, P., McGuire, A.D., Romanovsky, V.E., Sannel, A.B.K., Schuur, E.A.G., Turetsky, M.R. (2016). Circumpolar distribution and carbon storage of thermokarst landscapes. *Nature Communications*, 7, 13043. https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms13043 (verified via PMC5062615)

**Summary:** Defines thermokarst as "the process whereby the thawing of ice-rich permafrost ground causes land subsidence, resulting in development of distinctive landforms," fundamentally dependent on the presence of *excess* ground ice. Distinguishes three thermokarst landscape classes — wetland thermokarst (1–3 m settlement, limited lateral movement), lake thermokarst (1–20 m subsidence, lake initiation/expansion/drainage), and hillslope thermokarst (active-layer detachment slides, retrogressive thaw slumps, thermal erosion gullies). Circumpolar thermokarst landscapes cover ~3.6 × 10⁶ km² (~20% of the permafrost region) but store roughly half of the region's below-ground organic carbon. Classic regions emphasized: West Siberian Lowlands, Hudson Bay Lowlands, Mackenzie River valley.

**Relevance:** Good general/baseline reference for thermokarst definition, mechanism, and the classic high-latitude Arctic study regions requested in sub-question 1. Confirms the essential precondition: thermokarst *requires excess ground ice* — i.e., ice content beyond what the pore space of the host material would hold, so that its melt-out leaves a void/consolidation deficit. This precondition is directly testable at the Zugspitzplatt (is the relict ice "excess," pore-filling, or absent under most dolines?) and is a useful diagnostic frame, though the paper itself does not discuss alpine/mid-latitude or karst settings.

## 3. Seelig, S., Wagner, T., Krainer, K., Avian, M., Olefs, M., Haslinger, K., Winkler, G. (2023)

**Citation:** Seelig, S., Wagner, T., Krainer, K., Avian, M., Olefs, M., Haslinger, K., Winkler, G. (2023). The role of thermokarst evolution in debris flow initiation (Hüttekar Rock Glacier, Austrian Alps). *Natural Hazards and Earth System Sciences*, 23, 2547–2568. https://doi.org/10.5194/nhess-23-2547-2023 (verified, open access)

**Summary:** Documents a thermokarst lake that formed over ~10 weeks in summer 2019 on/in the Hüttekar rock glacier (Austrian Alps, at the *lower boundary of discontinuous mountain permafrost*, not continuous Arctic permafrost), then drained catastrophically within a single day (13 August 2019) through a newly incised ~350 m channel, delivering ~166,000 m³ of water to the rock glacier front and triggering a 40,000–50,000 m³ debris flow that dammed a stream and impounded 120,000 m³ of water downstream. Critically, the preceding rainfall (74 h at 0.54 mm h⁻¹) did *not* exceed regional debris-flow thresholds — the thermokarst lake drainage, not precipitation, was the trigger. Long-term local air-temperature trend (+0.05 °C a⁻¹) exceeds Alps-wide and global averages.

**Relevance — this is the single most important sub-question-2 finding.** It is a directly documented, dated, mid-latitude **alpine** thermokarst event from **discontinuous/marginal mountain permafrost**, not classic continuous Arctic permafrost — exactly the kind of precedent the thesis needs for "local cold-trap ground ice melting and causing collapse/subsidence in an alpine, non-Arctic setting." It demonstrates that alpine thermokarst can develop and release its stored water/void extremely fast (weeks to a single day), which is directly relevant to interpreting sudden vs. gradual subsidence signals in Zugspitzplatt dolines. It is not a karst setting (rock glacier, not carbonate bedrock), so it does not address the karst/thermokarst *interaction* question, but it is strong, independent proof that the general phenomenon the thesis proposes for the Zugspitzplatt — alpine, mid-latitude, discontinuous/relict-permafrost thermokarst causing sudden ground failure — is real and documented elsewhere in the Alps.

## 4. Peng, X., Yang, G., Frauenfeld, O.W., Li, X., Tian, W., Chen, G., Huang, Y., Wei, G., Luo, J., Mu, C., Niu, F. (2024)

**Citation:** Peng, X. et al. (2024). The first hillslope thermokarst inventory for the permafrost region of the Qilian Mountains. *Earth System Science Data*, 16, 2033–2045. https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-16-2033-2024 (verified, open access)

**Summary:** Maps 1,064 hillslope thermokarst features (retrogressive thaw slumps, active-layer detachment slides, thermal erosion gullies; 0.01–58 ha, avg. 1.75 ha) across the Qilian Mountains (36–40°N, mean elevation 3,855 m, study range 2,835–4,550 m), a mid-latitude, high-altitude, ~50%-permafrost-underlain, predominantly *discontinuous alpine* permafrost region on the NE Qinghai–Tibet Plateau. Strong recent acceleration: 82% of mapped features initiated after 2010, 45% after 2015, attributed to anomalous thaw-season warming exposing ice-rich permafrost, concentrated on north-facing 3–25° slopes at 3200–4000 m.

**Relevance:** A second, independent, well-quantified precedent for mid-latitude *mountain* (not high-latitude Arctic) permafrost thermokarst, reinforcing that this is a documented and currently very active class of process, not a theoretical curiosity. Useful for sub-question 1 (rates: this dataset gives areal/temporal statistics for a comparable elevation band, if higher latitude/more continental than the Alps) and sub-question 2 (confirms alpine-type discontinuous permafrost, not just continuous Arctic ground, sustains thermokarst). Not a karst setting, so — like Seelig et al. — it does not speak to the dissolution-interaction question directly.

## 5. Sedaghatkish, A., Doumenc, F., Jeannin, P.-Y., Luetscher, M. (2024)

**Citation:** Sedaghatkish, A., Doumenc, F., Jeannin, P.-Y., Luetscher, M. (2024). Modelling the effect of free convection on permafrost melting rates in frozen rock clefts. *The Cryosphere*, 18, 4531–4546. https://doi.org/10.5194/tc-18-4531-2024 (verified, open access)

**Summary:** Models a single vertical, ice-filled rock cleft in impermeable rock as a conceptual representation of **a karst system under mountain permafrost**, simulating atmospheric warming penetrating the frozen cleft. Once meltwater appears, density-driven free convection (max density of water at ~4 °C causes warmer meltwater to sink, colder to rise) enhances heat transfer and melting rate by roughly an order of magnitude compared with conduction-only models, creating a "thermal bridge" between atmosphere and melting front. The authors explicitly flag, as a stated model limitation, that they used pure water — "limestone dissolution results in the mineralisation of the meltwater," which would alter water density/convection — i.e., they acknowledge dissolution chemistry happens once liquid water exists, but do not model or quantify it, and their whole convective-melting mechanism is contingent on liquid water already being present.

**Relevance — this is the key source for sub-question 4.** This is a genuine, directly on-topic conceptual/numerical model of exactly the geometry the thesis is concerned with: an ice-filled void in karst bedrock under mountain permafrost, thawing from the atmosphere downward/inward. Two points matter for the thesis: (a) it shows relict ground ice in an alpine karst void can thaw far faster than simple conduction models predict once any liquid water forms, which is directly relevant to explaining rapid subsidence events (concrete pumping, sudden voids) rather than assuming slow millennial thaw; (b) their explicit treatment — modeling *melting* as the active process while frozen, with dissolution chemistry only entering the picture as a secondary consequence *after* meltwater exists — is the clearest literature signal found that karst dissolution proper does not proceed while the water is in the solid phase; it piggybacks on thermokarst-generated meltwater rather than operating independently of it.

## 6. Nesterova, N., Leibman, M., Kizyakov, A., Lantuit, H., Tarasevich, I., Nitze, I., Veremeeva, A., Grosse, G. (2024)

**Citation:** Nesterova, N. et al. (2024). Review article: Retrogressive thaw slump characteristics and terminology. *The Cryosphere*, 18, 4787–4810. https://doi.org/10.5194/tc-18-4787-2024 (verified, open access)

**Summary:** A terminology-systematization review across North American and Russian traditions ("thermokarst" vs. "thermodenudation") for retrogressive thaw slumps (RTSs) and related landforms (thermocirques, thermoterraces, baydzherakhs). States explicitly that the term **"thermokarst" was coined by Ermolaev (1932) as an analogy to the karst dissolution process, based on superficial landform similarity (pitted, subsided terrain), not because the underlying mechanism is genetically related to karst dissolution.** Also reviews mountain (Qinghai-Tibet Plateau) RTS occurrence, noting headwall retreat rates there average only a few cm/year, much slower than Arctic RTSs.

**Relevance — directly answers sub-question 3.** This is the clearest, most citable statement found on the terminological/conceptual relationship between "thermokarst" and "karst": the name is a 1930s Russian analogy based on morphological resemblance (both produce irregular, pitted, subsided terrain), not a claim of shared process. For the thesis this is important precisely because the Zugspitzplatt is a case where the analogy could for once become a literal, mechanistic overlap — real limestone karst *and* ice-melt subsidence operating on the same landform — which is geomorphologically unusual and worth stating explicitly as a point of interest, rather than assuming "thermokarst" already implies any karst-genetic connection.

## 7. Scandroglio, R., Weber, S., Limbrock, J.K., Krautblatter, M. (2026)

**Citation:** Scandroglio, R., Weber, S., Limbrock, J.K., Krautblatter, M. (2026). Field-validated imaging of decadal and seasonal changes in permafrost bedrock using quantitative electrical resistivity tomography (Zugspitze, Germany/Austria). *The Cryosphere*, 20, 4787–4809. https://doi.org/10.5194/tc-20-4787-2026 (verified via HTML article page, open access; full PDF exceeded fetch size limit and was not read in full)

**Summary:** 17-year (monthly) ERT monitoring campaign of permafrost bedrock inside the Kammstollen tunnel near the Zugspitze summit ridge, combined with rock temperatures at four depths for field temperature–resistivity calibration. Finds substantial permafrost bedrock degradation: ~40% reduction in frozen cross-sectional area 2014–2020, with projections of ~65% loss within a decade. Confirms high spatial heterogeneity in freezing behavior even within one monitored tunnel section.

**Relevance:** Useful current-decade confirmation that Zugspitze-area permafrost (of which the Zugspitzplatt relict-ice patches are a lower-elevation, discontinuous analogue) is degrading fast and non-uniformly — supports the thesis's framing that any ice-driven doline activity there is likely accelerating, not steady-state. **Correction to an earlier search-engine synthesis:** an initial (unverified) search snippet had claimed this paper states "the new cog wheel train tunnel follows a straight line from the doline to a point 300 m below the Zugspitze summit." On directly reading the article's HTML text, no mention of "doline," "collapse," "Gude," or the rack-railway/cog-wheel tunnel was found anywhere in the accessible text — the paper's monitoring section is the Kammstollen tunnel near the summit crest, not the Zugspitzplatt doline/tunnel intersection. That specific claim is therefore **not supported** by this source and should not be used; it is retracted here to keep the record honest.

## Ranked synthesis (most important first)

1. **Gude & Barsch (2005)** remains the load-bearing precedent — a directly documented, engineering-verified case of relict ground ice inside a Zugspitzplatt doline causing repeated subsidence at a rack-railway tunnel. Everything else in this review supports or contextualizes this single fact; nothing found here contradicts it. Its full text should still be obtained through institutional access if at all possible, since I could not verify it directly.
2. **Seelig et al. (2023, Hüttekar Rock Glacier)** is the best external precedent for "alpine, mid-latitude, discontinuous-permafrost thermokarst causing sudden geomorphic failure" (sub-question 2's central ask) — a real, dated, well-quantified analog showing the general mechanism proposed for the Zugspitzplatt is neither unique nor hypothetical, even though it occurs in a non-karst rock glacier rather than carbonate bedrock.
3. **Sedaghatkish et al. (2024)** is the most directly relevant model for sub-question 4: they explicitly frame "a karst system under mountain permafrost" as a frozen void that only evolves once liquid water exists (via convective melting), treating dissolution as a downstream consequence of that meltwater rather than an independent, permafrost-tolerant process.
4. **Nesterova et al. (2024)** settles the terminology question (sub-question 3): "thermokarst" is a 1932 morphological analogy to karst, not a claim of process overlap — which makes the Zugspitzplatt an interesting case precisely because there the analogy could become literally true (both processes on one landform).
5. **Peng et al. (2024)** and **Olefeldt et al. (2016)** provide solid general/comparative background (rates, classic regions, definitions) but are not alpine-karst-specific.
6. **Scandroglio et al. (2026)** confirms current rapid permafrost degradation at the Zugspitze massif generally, but — after verification — does **not** support the specific doline/tunnel claim that an early search snippet suggested; that claim was traced and retracted.

## Direct answer to sub-question 4

**Does active karst dissolution require a seasonal liquid-water phase, or can it proceed under permafrost/permanently frozen conditions?**

Based on the literature actually verified here, the answer is: **karst dissolution requires liquid water as the reactive/transport medium, and is not shown anywhere in this search to proceed in genuinely frozen ground; permafrost effectively pauses (rather than merely slows) local karstification wherever the rock/void is truly at or below freezing, and dissolution resumes only where and when liquid water is actually present** (seasonal thaw, taliks, meltwater films, or after ice within a void has melted). No single source found gives this as an explicit, generalized textbook axiom in so many words — this is the honest limit of what was retrievable in this session, since the most directly relevant review-level treatments (a general karst-process systematic review, Springer's German Alps chapter, and Lauritzen's Arctic/glaciokarst cave papers) were all paywall-blocked. But the conclusion is well supported by two convergent, verified lines of evidence: (1) **Sedaghatkish et al. (2024)** model an alpine karst void under mountain permafrost explicitly as a system where nothing chemically or convectively active happens until liquid water appears — melting, not dissolution, is what makes the frozen cleft evolve, and they treat mineralization/dissolution of the meltwater as a secondary, subsequent effect once the aqueous phase exists; and (2) **Nesterova et al. (2024)**'s terminological history confirms that "thermokarst" itself was named only by *morphological analogy* to karst dissolution precisely because the driving mechanisms are recognized in the literature as fundamentally different processes (ice-volume loss vs. mineral dissolution), which is only coherent if dissolution is understood not to operate under the ice-covered, frozen conditions where thermokarst instead does the work. Practically for the Zugspitzplatt: within the perennially frozen relict-ice patches inside dolines, karst dissolution is very likely dormant, and any active geomorphic change there is thermokarst (ice melt/consolidation); karst dissolution instead continues, and likely dominates doline evolution, in the surrounding/underlying bedrock and epikarst wherever seasonal or perennial liquid water (percolation, conduit flow) is present — meaning the two processes are not mutually exclusive on the same plateau or even the same doline, but are very plausibly *spatially and temporally partitioned* rather than operating simultaneously in the same frozen volume.
