# Literature Review: Quaternary Glacial History, Structural Geology, and Regional Context — Zugspitze / Wetterstein Mountains

Compiled 2026-09-28 in support of the thesis on Zugspitzplatt collapse dolines (karst dissolution vs. thermokarst hypothesis). Search conducted via web search and web-fetch of publisher pages, ResearchGate, Copernicus/EGU open-access journals, and institutional repositories. Access level (full text vs. abstract/snippet only) is noted for every source, since several key publishers (ScienceDirect, Springer, Tandfonline, some ResearchGate pages) returned HTTP 403 and could only be characterized through search-result snippets, not by directly reading the article. Where that is the case it is stated explicitly — treat those summaries as provisional and verify against the primary text before citing in the thesis.

## 1. Quaternary / Holocene Glacial Chronology

### 1.1 Hirtlreiter, G. (1992). *Spät- und postglaziale Gletscherschwankungen im Wettersteingebirge und seiner Umgebung* [Late- and postglacial glacier fluctuations in the Wetterstein Mountains and surroundings]. Münchener Geographische Abhandlungen, Reihe B, Vol. 15, 176 pp. (Geobuch-Verlag, München).

**Access:** Catalog record (Heidelberg university library, Google Books) and secondary citations only; could not obtain full text online. This is a pre-digital monograph/dissertation, not indexed by the major aggregators I could reach.

**Summary:** A dedicated, site-specific reconstruction of Late Glacial and Holocene glacier fluctuations in the Wetterstein massif (the very range containing the Zugspitzplatt), based on geomorphological mapping of moraines and glacier forefield features. Secondary sources citing it place the historic ("Little Ice Age") maximum of the Plattachferner — the glacier that once covered almost the entire Zugspitzplatt — at around 1774–1820 AD, at roughly 300 ha, after which it broke up into the Nördlicher and Südlicher Schneeferner plus smaller firn patches. This is consistent with, and appears to be a primary source underlying, the historical baseline used in Hagg & Mayer (2026) and Mayer et al. (2021, below).

**Relevance:** This is the single most important *specifically local* source for glacial-chronology topic #1 identified in this search. It is the standard reference for late-glacial/Holocene glacier behavior in the Wetterstein massif and very likely contains (or was the primary basis for) any mapped Löbben- or Egesen-stadial-equivalent moraine positions in the Reintal/Zugspitzplatt catchment, i.e. exactly the kind of evidence needed to bracket when the plateau was ice-free versus re-glaciated in the Holocene. **Strong recommendation: obtain this via interlibrary loan / LMU or TUM library — it could not be read here and its specific stadial-by-stadial content (Egesen? Löbben?) could not be verified**, only its existence and general subject matter.

### 1.2 Mayer, C., Weber, M., Wendt, A., & Hagg, W. (2021). Die bayerischen Gletscher, die verbliebenen Eisreserven Deutschlands [The Bavarian glaciers, Germany's remaining ice reserves]. *Polarforschung*, 89, 1–7. https://doi.org/10.5194/polf-89-1-2021

**Access:** Read in full (open access, Copernicus/AWI journal).

**Summary:** Documents the area/volume evolution of Bavaria's five remaining glaciers (all in the Wetterstein/Zugspitze and Watzmann areas) from systematic measurements starting 1949 to 2018. Confirms the Little Ice Age maximum (~1850) at just over 4 km² total glaciated area in the German Alps, with the Plattachferner alone at ~300 ha around 1774. By 2018 the five glaciers covered only 44.6 ha (3.95 million m³), having lost 62% of volume between 2009–2018 alone. Concludes the remaining ice will be "down to a few remnants" within decades — a prediction now borne out by Hagg & Mayer (2026).

**Relevance:** Same author group as the already-known Hagg & Mayer (2026) paper; this is effectively its direct predecessor and gives the pre-2018 quantitative baseline. Useful for the Study Area section's discussion of recent deglaciation, and as context establishing that the *historic* (LIA) glaciation of the Zugspitzplatt was itself a comparatively modest re-advance (~300 ha) sitting on top of a plateau that had already been an established karst surface for a much longer period — i.e., it does not represent a major re-carving event, unlike a full Egesen-scale advance would. Good citation to pair with Hagg & Mayer (2026) in the Study Area/recent-history section.

### 1.3 Ivy-Ochs, S., Kerschner, H., Reuther, A., Preusser, F., Heine, K., Maisch, M., Kubik, P.W., & Schlüchter, C. (2008). Chronology of the last glacial cycle in the European Alps. *Journal of Quaternary Science*, 23(6), 559–573.

**Access:** Abstract/search-snippet only — full text is paywalled (Wiley) and no open-access copy could be located. Not read in full.

**Summary (from abstract-level information):** The standard regional synthesis of Alpine glacial chronology from the Eemian through the Holocene (115–11 ka), integrating radiocarbon and cosmogenic (10Be, 36Cl) exposure ages across the Alps. Establishes the now-standard stadial framework (LGM maximum ~24–21 ka; Gschnitz, Clavadel/Senders, Daun, **Egesen** stadial ~12.2–11.3 ka tied to the Younger Dryas) that regional/local studies (including presumably Hirtlreiter 1992 and Grüger & Jerz) are calibrated against.

**Relevance:** Essential *regional* backbone reference for the Study Area/methods section whenever the thesis needs to name or date a stadial (e.g., interpreting the "Löbben" oscillation already cited via Grüger & Jerz, or discussing the Egesen stadial mentioned by Küfmann/Zech 2003 below). It is not Zugspitze-specific and adds no new site data, but it is the paper virtually every other source in this list implicitly cites for absolute ages — worth citing directly rather than only through secondary works. **Recommend securing full text via institutional access before final citation**, since I could not verify page-specific numbers beyond the volume/date.

### 1.4 Gap identified: no cosmogenic exposure dating (10Be/36Cl) of the Zugspitzplatt surface or adjacent moraines

Despite targeted searching (multiple query variants combining "Zugspitze"/"Wetterstein" with "cosmogenic", "10Be", "36Cl", "exposure dating", "moraine"), **no published cosmogenic surface-exposure study of the Zugspitzplatt plateau or its immediate moraines was found.** This appears to be a genuine gap in the literature, not a search failure — the regional Alpine cosmogenic-dating literature (Ivy-Ochs et al. and many follow-on papers) is extensive elsewhere in the Alps (Dolomites, Tyrol, Swiss Alps) but does not appear to have been applied to this specific massif. This is worth stating explicitly in the thesis: the timing of Zugspitzplatt's deglaciation after the LGM, and of any Holocene re-advances, currently rests on geomorphological mapping (Hirtlreiter 1992) and one radiocarbon date from doline fill (Grüger & Jerz, already known), not on absolute cosmogenic surface ages. That is itself a defensible motivation for why remote-sensing-based relative-age/process inference (the thesis's own approach) is valuable here.

## 2. Structural / Tectonic Geology of the Wetterstein Massif

### 2.1 Duplicate check: Ortner, H. & Kilian, S. (2022). Thrust tectonics in the Wetterstein and Mieming mountains, and a new tectonic subdivision of the Northern Calcareous Alps of western Austria and southern Germany. *International Journal of Earth Sciences*, 111, 543–571. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00531-021-02128-3

I want to flag explicitly: this **is the same paper already listed in the existing project literature** as "Ortner & Kilian (2022, structural geology/fault mapping of the Wetterstein/Mieming massif)". I confirmed the exact citation (journal, volume, pages, DOI) via the University of Innsbruck's own publication list. No separate/duplicate 2022 Ortner & Kilian paper exists — several search hits for "Ortner Wetterstein Mieming fault" simply pointed back to this one paper. Not counted as new.

### 2.2 Kilian, S., Ortner, H., & Schneider-Muntau, B. (2021). Buckle folding in the Northern Calcareous Alps — Field observations and numeric experiments. *Journal of Structural Geology*, 150, 104416. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsg.2021.104416

**Access:** Abstract read via search snippet (ScienceDirect full text paywalled/403; direct PDF at uibk.ac.at exceeded fetch size limit).

**Summary:** A combined field + numerical-modelling study of large-scale buckle folding in the western Northern Calcareous Alps fold-and-thrust belt. Simplifies the relevant stratigraphy to three mechanical layers: an incompetent evaporitic (salt-bearing) décollement layer at the base, an overlying **up to 3-km-thick competent layer** (this is the Wetterstein/Hauptdolomit carbonate megasequence that includes the Wettersteinkalk), and an incompetent top layer. Shows the weak evaporitic base is a prerequisite for folding of the thick competent carbonate package above it.

**Relevance:** Directly useful for characterizing the mechanical behavior of the Wetterstein limestone as a competent unit — i.e., it deforms by brittle fracture/faulting rather than ductile flow, consistent with the joint/fracture-controlled karst development assumed in the doline-causation hypothesis. Establishes that folding-related fracture/joint sets in the Wetterstein carbonate package are mechanically predictable from the layer's thickness and the underlying décollement — potentially useful if the thesis wants to argue that regional fold structure (not just later brittle faulting) set up the joint fabric that dolines now exploit.

### 2.3 Ortner, H. & Kilian, S. (2019). Structural evidence of in-sequence and out-of-sequence thrusting in the Karwendel mountains and the tectonic subdivision of the western Northern Calcareous Alps. *Austrian Journal of Earth Sciences*, 112(1), 62–83. https://doi.org/10.17738/ajes.2019.0005

**Access:** Citation confirmed; could not retrieve full body text (redirect chain on the open-access AJES/Sciendo host did not resolve to readable content in this session). Summary below is from search-snippet level only.

**Summary:** Companion/precursor study to Ortner & Kilian (2022), analyzing the Karwendel range (immediately north/northeast of the Wetterstein, across the Isar/Inn valley) using the same in-sequence vs. out-of-sequence thrusting framework, refining the tectonic subdivision of the western Northern Calcareous Alps as a whole.

**Relevance:** Same author pair, same regional tectonic model as the already-known Ortner & Kilian (2022) Wetterstein/Mieming paper — this is the sister paper for the neighboring range. Relevant mainly for framing: it shows Ortner & Kilian's tectonic subdivision approach is applied consistently across the western NCA, which strengthens confidence in using their Wetterstein-specific fault/thrust map as the structural basis for the thesis. Lower priority than 2.1 — cite only if the thesis needs the broader regional tectonic framework rather than Wetterstein-specific detail.

### 2.4 van Kooten, W.S.M.T., Ortner, H., Willingshofer, E., Sokoutis, D., Gruber, A., & Sausgruber, T. (2024). Fold localization at pre-existing normal faults: field observations and analogue modelling of the Achental structure, Northern Calcareous Alps, Austria. *Solid Earth*, 15, 91–120. https://doi.org/10.5194/se-15-91-2024

**Access:** Read reasonably fully (open access, EGU/Copernicus journal).

**Summary:** Field and analogue-modelling study showing that pre-existing Jurassic extensional (normal) faults in the Achental area (east of the Wetterstein, near Achensee) controlled the location and geometry of later Cretaceous NW-directed shortening structures — new thrusts formed antithetic to the old normal faults, localizing folding at basin-margin structures. Triassic carbonate platforms including Wetterstein limestone are discussed only as part of the general stratigraphic framework, not analyzed at the Zugspitze/Wetterstein locality itself.

**Relevance:** Not Zugspitze-specific — I want to be honest that on closer reading this paper's actual case study is a different structure (Achental), not the Wetterstein massif. Its value to the thesis is conceptual/methodological: it is strong, general evidence from the same authors' research group (Ortner et al.) that **inherited normal-fault and joint architecture from Mesozoic extension exerts first-order control on where later brittle structures — and by extension fracture networks — localize** in this exact rock package (Wetterstein-type Triassic carbonates) within the Northern Calcareous Alps. This supports (by analogy, not by direct evidence) the working hypothesis that doline locations on the Zugspitzplatt could be structurally controlled by inherited fracture/fault architecture rather than being randomly distributed. Use with appropriate caveats — it is an analogy from an adjacent structure, not a study of the Zugspitzplatt itself.

### 2.5 Ortner, H. & Bitterlich, L. (2016). The Zugspitze cross section and the structure of the western Northern Calcareous Alps. Abstract Volume of GeoTirol2016, p. 248.

**Access:** Citation only (conference abstract, via Innsbruck TRG publication list); no expanded text exists to fetch — it is a one-page abstract.

**Summary/Relevance:** A conference abstract presenting a structural cross-section specifically through the Zugspitze (rather than the Wetterstein/Mieming mountains generally). This is the closest thing found to a Zugspitze-specific structural cross-section, predating and presumably feeding into Ortner & Kilian (2022). Being only an abstract, it has essentially no standalone citable content beyond confirming that a Zugspitze-specific cross-section exists in Ortner's work — the 2022 paper is the full, citable version. Not worth citing separately from Ortner & Kilian (2022) unless the thesis specifically needs to reference the earlier presentation.

## 3. Regional / Geomorphological Context for the Study Area

### 3.1 Küfmann, C. [as "Zech, W. & Küfmann, C." per some indexes — author list could not be fully confirmed, see note] (2003). Soil types and eolian dust in high-mountainous karst of the Northern Calcareous Alps (Zugspitzplatt, Wetterstein Mountains, Germany). *Catena*, 53(3), 211–227.

**Access:** Search-snippet level only (ScienceDirect and ResearchGate both returned 403/blocked on direct fetch). Not read in full — treat details below as provisional.

**Summary:** A pedological survey of the Zugspitzplatt's alpine (2000–2350 m) and subnival (2350–2600 m) zones. Reports that the extremely pure limestone (CaCO₃+MgCO₃ ≥ 95–98%) leaves almost no residue for autochthonous soil formation, so instead of normal A-B-C profiles, thin organic (folic/rendzic) horizons develop directly on rock, except where a distinctive ~30-cm-thick brown, loess-loam-like cambisol layer occurs across large parts of the alpine zone. Mineralogical/heavy-mineral evidence traces this cover to **eolian dust sourced from the Central Alps' crystalline massifs**, and the authors tie its deposition to **late-glacial loess deposition during the Egesen stadial (Younger Dryas)**, with present-day (foehn-driven) dust input continuing as a smaller ongoing contribution.

**Relevance:** Important for two reasons. First, it is the standard reference for the surficial soil/sediment cover of the Zugspitzplatt — directly relevant to interpreting UAV/multispectral signatures of doline fill material, since it establishes that fine-grained, non-carbonate sediment on the plateau is very likely allochthonous loess-type dust rather than local dissolution residue. This matters for the causation question: doline fills of "loess-like" material (also noted by Grüger & Jerz for the dated doline) may simply reflect that this is the only fine sediment available to accumulate in any depression, independent of whether the depression itself is a dissolution or thermokarst feature. Second, the tie to the Egesen stadial gives an independent, non-glaciological terminus-post-quem: eolian mantle deposition (and by extension, stable land-surface conditions allowing it to accumulate) was underway on the plateau by the end of the Pleistocene/Younger Dryas, consistent with the plateau having been ice-free and karst-active well before the mid-Holocene. **Note on author attribution:** search results consistently gave "Küfmann, C." as sole/lead author for the Catena 2003 paper in bibliographic listings, but the title closely parallels later co-authored (Zech et al.) soil papers on the same site — confirm the exact author list from the actual article before citing, as I could not verify it directly.

### 3.2 Küfmann, C. (2008). Are Cambisols in Alpine Karst Autochthonous or Eolian in Origin? *Arctic, Antarctic, and Alpine Research*, 40(3), 506–518.

**Access:** Search-snippet level only (Tandfonline/BioOne both blocked direct fetch).

**Summary:** A two-year pedological study of 30 soil profiles on Triassic and Cretaceous limestones on the **Reiteralpe** paleosurface (1600–2000 m), a different karst plateau in the Northern Calcareous Alps (Berchtesgaden area, not the Wetterstein). Finds that reddish B-horizon soils are largely autochthonous (residue from limestone dissolution, with color/thickness controlled by iron-clay content of the parent rock), while topsoils carry a substantial eolian addition, including winter Saharan dust delivered by foehn winds.

**Relevance:** On closer inspection this paper is **not about the Zugspitzplatt** — it is a different massif entirely (Reiteralpe, ~100 km east). I am flagging this explicitly rather than forcing a connection: it is a useful methodological analog (same author, same eolian-vs-autochthonous soil question, same general Northern Calcareous Alps karst setting, same dust sources) and could be cited as supporting regional context for the eolian-dust interpretation in Küfmann's own Zugspitzplatt paper (3.1) and in Küfmann (2013, already known), but it should not be cited as if it reports data from the Zugspitzplatt itself.

### 3.3 Vogel, W. & Zaenker, S. (2008). Die Karstgebiete der Bayerischen Alpen: Übersicht, Gefährdung und erforderliche Schutzmaßnahmen [The karst areas of the Bavarian Alps: overview, threats, and required protection measures]. *Jahrbuch des Vereins zum Schutz der Bergwelt*, 73.

**Access:** Search-snippet level only (source PDF exceeded the 10 MB fetch limit and could not be retrieved in full).

**Summary:** A regional overview/inventory of karst areas across the Bavarian Alps, written for a mountain-conservation yearbook (not a primary research journal, but authored by active cavers/karst specialists). Identifies the Zugspitzplatt and the neighboring Leutascher Platt as the largest karst areas in the Bavarian Alps, notes >70 documented caves on the Zugspitzplatt alone, and states that the karst surface was shaped by Ice Age glacial overprinting with many near-surface cavities subsequently filled or sealed by sediment.

**Relevance:** A useful non-technical but authoritative regional-overview citation for the Study Area section — good for orienting the reader to the scale of karstification (cave density) on the plateau and for the general statement that the karst surface has been glacially overprinted, which is directly relevant background for disentangling "which features are relict glacial/periglacial forms versus active dissolution features" — precisely the thesis's central question. Given the venue (a conservation-society yearbook, not peer-reviewed), treat as a secondary/overview source rather than a primary research citation, and prefer to cross-check any specific numeric claims (e.g. "70 caves") against a peer-reviewed source if one is available (e.g. Grüger & Jerz, or regional speleological literature).

### 3.4 Scandroglio, R., Weber, S., Limbrock, J.K., & Krautblatter, M. (2026). Field-validated imaging of decadal and seasonal changes in permafrost bedrock using quantitative electrical resistivity tomography (Zugspitze, Germany/Austria). *The Cryosphere*, 20, 4787–4809. https://doi.org/10.5194/tc-20-4787-2026

**Access:** Read reasonably fully (open access, EGU/Copernicus journal).

**Summary:** This is the direct, decade-plus follow-up to the already-known Krautblatter et al. (2010) ERT feasibility study, extending the monitoring record to **17 years of continuous monthly ERT measurements (2007–2024)** on the Zugspitze summit ridge (~2800 m a.s.l.). Key findings: over 2014–2023, a **40% reduction in permanently frozen surface area**, resistivity decline from 37 to 28 kΩm, ~1°C rock-temperature increase, and active-layer degradation rates up to −4.2 kΩm/yr, with steeper decline in summer than winter. Linear extrapolation suggests roughly **65% of remaining permafrost could be lost within a decade**. Newly-thawed fracture networks are identified as a mechanism that may *enhance* further heat transport into the rock mass (a positive feedback). Critically, the authors' permafrost distribution modelling places the **lower elevation limit of (relatively warm) permafrost at roughly 2350 m a.s.l. on shaded/north-facing slopes** under current conditions.

**Relevance:** This is arguably the single most important new source found in this search for the causation question directly. It is a rigorous, current (2026), directly comparable update of a paper already in the thesis's literature base, and it provides a **quantitative modern lower elevation bound for permafrost (~2350 m)** that sits *above* most of the Zugspitzplatt's own elevation range (~2000–2350 m alpine zone per Küfmann 2003). This is important supporting evidence for the thermokarst hypothesis in a specific, falsifiable way: if present-day permafrost (let alone permafrost during any warmer Holocene interval) is essentially confined to shaded terrain above ~2350 m, then **relict ground ice at or below that elevation on the plateau proper would have to be a inherited, likely Pleistocene-age feature that has since been buried/insulated from the modern climate signal** (e.g., under doline-fill sediment or coarse debris) rather than something in current equilibrium with the atmosphere — exactly the kind of process argument the thesis needs to make explicit when proposing thermokarst as a mechanism at ~2000 m elevation. Recommend using this paper's elevation/degradation-rate numbers directly alongside Krautblatter et al. (2010) and Gude & Barsch (2005) when discussing the plausibility (or implausibility, depending on framing) of present-day vs. relict ground ice at the doline sites.

### 3.5 Lindner, F. et al. (2021). Seasonal Freeze-Thaw Cycles and Permafrost Degradation on Mt. Zugspitze (German/Austrian Alps) Revealed by Single-Station Seismic Monitoring. *Geophysical Research Letters*, 48. https://doi.org/10.1029/2021GL094659

**Access:** Search-snippet level only (Wiley/AGU full text returned 403).

**Summary:** Uses ambient seismic noise from cable-car traffic recorded continuously by a single seismic station at the Zugspitze summit (2006–2021) as a passive proxy for near-surface seismic velocity, which tracks rock freeze/thaw state. Finds clear seasonal seismic-velocity cycles from freeze-thaw plus a **long-term velocity decrease attributed to gradual permafrost degradation** over the observation period.

**Relevance:** A methodologically interesting complementary line of evidence (independent of ERT) for ongoing permafrost degradation at the Zugspitze summit, reinforcing the Scandroglio et al. (2026) picture. Like the ERT studies, this is summit-ridge data (not plateau-level), so its direct applicability to the Zugspitzplatt itself is by extrapolation only — worth a citation in a permafrost-degradation-rate discussion but should not be over-stated as plateau-specific evidence.

### 3.6 Hagg, W. & Krautblatter, M. (2025). Glacial, Periglacial and the Recent Permafrost Domains in the German Alps. Chapter 27 in: Lehmkuhl, F., Böse, M., & Krautblatter, M. (eds.), *Landscapes and Landforms of Germany*, Springer.

**Access:** Citation confirmed via search; full text is paywalled (Springer, login redirect) and could not be read.

**Summary/Relevance:** A recent (2025), authoritative German-Alps-wide regional-overview book chapter co-authored by two of the researchers already central to this thesis's literature base (Hagg, co-author of the newly-found Hagg & Mayer 2026 paper; Krautblatter, author of the already-known 2010 ERT study and the new Scandroglio et al. 2026 update above). Given its title and authorship, it almost certainly synthesizes glacial, periglacial, and permafrost domains across the German Alps including the Zugspitze/Wetterstein area, and would be an efficient single citation for the thesis's Study Area section to establish the broader German-Alps glacial/periglacial/permafrost context in one place. **Recommend obtaining via institutional library access** (TUM, where Krautblatter is based, almost certainly has it) — I could not verify its Zugspitze-specific content directly.

## Ranked Synthesis (most important first)

1. **Scandroglio, Weber, Limbrock & Krautblatter (2026), *The Cryosphere*** — the most directly useful new source. Gives a current, quantitative, published lower-elevation bound (~2350 m) for present-day Zugspitze permafrost and a measured degradation rate, letting the thesis make a specific, citable argument about why any ground ice at the ~2000 m Zugspitzplatt would have to be relict rather than in equilibrium with today's climate — the crux of the thermokarst hypothesis.

2. **Hirtlreiter (1992), Münchener Geographische Abhandlungen** — the key missing piece for topic #1: a Wetterstein-specific Late Glacial/Holocene glacier-fluctuation reconstruction. Could not be read here (pre-digital monograph); tracking down a physical/library copy should be a priority, since it likely contains the actual mapped evidence (or absence of evidence) for Löbben/Egesen-type re-advances reaching the Zugspitzplatt itself.

3. **Küfmann/Zech (2003), *Catena*, "Soil types and eolian dust..."** — establishes that fine sediment infilling depressions on the plateau is predominantly Late Glacial (Egesen-age) and modern eolian dust, not autochthonous dissolution residue. Directly relevant to interpreting doline-fill signatures in the thesis's remote-sensing data, and gives an independent terminus-post-quem for stable, karst-active surface conditions on the plateau by the Younger Dryas.

4. **The absence of any cosmogenic (10Be/36Cl) exposure-dating study of the Zugspitzplatt itself** — a genuine, citable gap in the literature worth stating explicitly in the thesis's framing of what is and isn't independently dated at this site.

5. **Kilian, Ortner & Schneider-Muntau (2021), *JSG*, buckle folding** and **van Kooten et al. (2024), *Solid Earth*, Achental fault reactivation** — both reinforce, from the same research group as the already-known Ortner & Kilian (2022), that inherited Mesozoic extensional structure and fold mechanics control where later brittle deformation (and by extension fracture networks relevant to karst) localizes in Wetterstein-type carbonate packages — useful supporting/analogical evidence for a structurally-controlled doline-location hypothesis, though neither is a Zugspitze-specific case study.

6. **Mayer et al. (2021), *Polarforschung*** and confirmation that Ortner & Kilian's "Thrust tectonics..." and Gude & Barsch's "geomorphic hazards" papers are the *same* sources already in the project's literature base (not new) — useful for avoiding duplicate citation and for precise volume/page references, but lower priority as findings.

7. **Vogel & Zaenker (2008)** and **Hagg & Krautblatter (2025) book chapter** — solid general Study Area background/orientation sources, not primary research findings; recommended for the regional-overview paragraph rather than the causation argument itself.

8. **Küfmann (2008), *AAAR*, Reiteralpe cambisols** and **Lindner et al. (2021), *GRL*, seismic monitoring** — genuinely relevant but only by analogy (different plateau) or by extrapolation (summit-ridge, not plateau, permafrost data) respectively; cite with appropriate caveats rather than as direct Zugspitzplatt evidence.
